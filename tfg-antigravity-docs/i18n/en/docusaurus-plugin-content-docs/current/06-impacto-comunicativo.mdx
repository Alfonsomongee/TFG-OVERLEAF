# Socio-Communicative Impact and News Coverage Treatment (Chapter 6)

The communicative management of the April 28, 2025 blackout constituted a significant case of *crisis communication failure*: the inability of institutions to occupy the information space with verifiable technical messages. The voltage zero occurred at 12:33 CEST; the first official public statement did not take place until 18:00 CEST.

This vacuum was immediately filled by what the WHO has termed *infodemia*: propagation of unverified information in contexts of extreme uncertainty. Hypotheses of sabotage, cyberattacks, and atmospheric phenomena circulated globally before operators issued diagnosis.

The nature of the incident—a collapse due to overvoltage whose mechanics require knowledge of <GlossaryLink term="Estabilidad de tensión">voltage stability</GlossaryLink>, <GlossaryLink term="IBR">IBR</GlossaryLink>, and <GlossaryLink term="OLTC (On-Load Tap Changer)">OLTC</GlossaryLink>—made it especially vulnerable: technical complexity was inversely proportional to the accessibility of alternative explanations.

---

## Press Analysis: Technical Biases

### Critical Narrative Toward Institutional Management

Media outlets such as El Mundo and ABC articulated a narrative linking the collapse to energy management, interpreting it as a consequence of renewable penetration without sufficient backup. El Mundo connected the interconnection deficit to institutional responsibility. ABC focused analysis on rotational inertia, asserting lack of "nuclear energy," contradicting official records: the system had inertia $H = 2{,}3$ s, exceeding ENTSO-E recommendations.

### Press Favorable to Institutional Narrative

El País highlighted exchange capacity with France at 3%, below the European target of 15%, a technically consistent argument. **elDiario.es** noted reduced safety margins, coherent with <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power</GlossaryLink> deficit in the south. However, these coverages frequently omitted multicausal factors such as the <GlossaryLink term="Efecto Ferranti">Ferranti Effect</GlossaryLink> and <GlossaryLink term="Tap-Lag">Tap-Lag</GlossaryLink>.

### International Coverage

Reuters provided technical analysis on interconnections. Financial Times titled *"blamed on solar power dependency,"* attributing it to insufficient "firm power," contradictory to consensus: the system had reserves and nuclear reactors. The Telegraph suggested a government "experiment," refuted by fact-checkers.

---

## Social Media: Three Response Phases

### Phase 1 (0-6 hours): Acute Uncertainty

Absence of government communication favored misinformation. Hypotheses of sabotage and cyberattacks propagated easily. Citizen response coherent with *emergent norm theory*: customary humor as a mechanism of emotional coping with unprecedented disruption.

### Phase 2 (6-72 hours): Politicization

Consolidation of causal attribution narratives. Competing frames for causal explanation. Asymmetric selection of evidence: critical media emphasized renewable deficit/synchronous generation; favorable media emphasized operator error.

### Phase 3: Delayed Correction

Technical explanations arrived with reach significantly inferior to initial messages. Persistent gap between technical consensus and dominant media narrative.

---

## Conclusion

The incident was covered according to pre-existing interpretive frameworks, reducing a multicausal phenomenon to binary causal relationships. Critical media omitted meshing; favorable media omitted actual generator behavior. No bloc offered representation of <GlossaryLink term="IBR">multicausality according to IBR expertise</GlossaryLink>.

This polarization hinders sustained political consensus for identified technical reforms: P.O. 7.4 update, synchronous deployment, <GlossaryLink term="GFM (Grid-Forming)">grid-forming</GlossaryLink> transition.
