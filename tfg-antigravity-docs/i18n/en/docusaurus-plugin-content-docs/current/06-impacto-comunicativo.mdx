<GlitchTitle>Communicative Impact</GlitchTitle>

The blackout of April 28 generated a second crisis parallel to the electrical one: a communicative crisis whose consequences for the public understanding of the incident persist beyond the restoration of supply. This chapter analyzes how the media and social networks processed a highly complex technical phenomenon, and what gap opened between the technical consensus of the expert reports and the narrative that reached the Spanish public opinion.

## Institutional Communication Failure and the Information Vacuum

The voltage collapse occurred at 12:33 CEST. The first official public appearance by the President of the Government did not take place until 18:00 CEST — more than five hours later. During that interval, the hyperconnected ecosystem, suddenly deprived of electrical supply and digital access, processed the emergency in the absence of any institutional interpretive framework.

This vacuum was immediately filled by what specialized literature calls *crisis communication failure*: the inability of institutions to occupy the information space with verifiable technical messages in the critical interval following the incident. The consequence was an episode of infodemic: hypotheses of sabotage, Russian cyberattacks, anomalous atmospheric phenomena, and government experiments circulated globally before operators issued a preliminary diagnosis.

The nature of the incident made it especially vulnerable to this dynamic: a collapse due to overvoltage, whose mechanics require knowledge of voltage stability, IBR behavior, and OLTC dynamics, is, by definition, inversely proportional in accessibility to the alternative explanations that replaced it in the public sphere. Real technical complexity was displaced by simple, immediately assimilable narratives.

The direct consequence was the incorporation of the incident into the political debate on the energy transition before a verified technical diagnosis existed. For weeks, the Spanish public received causal explanations — "renewables caused the blackout," "nuclear plants were missing," "Europe abandoned Spain" — that the four technical reports do not unanimously support. The distance between the technical consensus and the dominant media narrative is, in itself, one of the consequences of April 28, with direct implications for society's ability to evaluate the regulatory reforms that the incident highlights.

## The Historical Mirror: The Northeast US Blackout (2003)

On August 14, 2003, a cascading failure left approximately 55 million people without electricity in eight US states and the Canadian province of Ontario. Economic losses were estimated between $4 and $10 billion. Twenty-two years later, the similarities with the Iberian blackout are unsettling, especially in the communicative sphere.

### Silent Alarm Failure and Operational Blindness

The 2003 collapse originated from a race condition in FirstEnergy's XA/21 energy management system in Ohio. The software failed silently: the alarms that should have alerted operators to overloaded lines never activated. For over an hour, human controllers were unaware that the grid was disintegrating as 345 kV lines heated, sagged, and came into contact with untrimmed trees.

In 2025, the equivalent was the unobservability of the 220 kV grid due to the Tap-Lag phenomenon: the OLTCs of the transformers, with their intentional mechanical delays, amplified the overvoltage towards the collector networks while REE's SCADA showed apparently normal voltage at 400 kV. In both cases, the human operator did not see the real threat until it was too late.

### Telecommunications Collapse and Civil Panic

On August 14, 2003, mobile phone networks saturated within minutes. Cell towers, lacking sufficient backup power, ceased to function. Even the 800 MHz emergency radios of the New York City Department of Health lost charge. The population was left incommunicado in the middle of a summer afternoon.

On April 28, 2025, digital dependence was even greater. The blackout left over 40 million smartphones without connection. The few that had battery power encountered saturated networks and crashed news portals. The irony: the medium most used to spread hoaxes — X (formerly Twitter) — was inaccessible to those without mobile data.

### Projection of Fear: From Terrorism to Cyberattack

Just two years after 9/11, the collective American psyche projected the fear of terrorism onto the 2003 blackout. During the first hours, television networks openly speculated about an Al Qaeda attack on the power grid. It wasn't until the next day that President George W. Bush officially ruled out terrorism.

In 2025, the equivalent was "Operation Matryoshka": the hypothesis of a Russian cyberattack on solar inverters went viral on X in less than two hours. REE and the Government took over five hours to rule it out. The historical lesson is clear: when authorities do not quickly fill the information vacuum, society fills it with its most immediate fears, whether terrorism or hybrid warfare.

### Lessons Not Learned

The final report of the 2003 blackout investigation (<InteractiveFootnote content="U.S.-Canada Power System Outage Task Force (2004). Available at: https://www.energy.gov" citation="U.S.-Canada Power System Outage Task Force, 2004, pp. 1–228">U.S.-Canada Power System Outage Task Force, 2004</InteractiveFootnote>) recommended:

- Improving operator training for alarm failure situations.
- Requiring backup power sources for critical communication towers.
- Establishing public communication protocols with predefined messages for the first 30 minutes of a crisis.

April 28, 2025, demonstrated that none of these lessons had been fully implemented in Southern Europe. The 22-year difference was not technological — it was a matter of institutional will.

## Press Analysis: Frames and Technical Biases

### Media Critical of Institutional Management

Outlets such as El Mundo, ABC, and La Razón articulated a narrative linking the collapse to the Government's energy policy, interpreting it as a foreseeable consequence of a transition that prioritized intermittent sources without guaranteeing sufficient backup mechanisms.

Media aligned with the official narrative shifted the focus towards REE's operational management, the shortcomings of the transmission grid, and inherited policies. The articulating premise was that the collapse did not constitute a failure of decarbonization but rather the result of infrastructure not modernized at the necessary pace.

### International Coverage: European Structural Framing

The international press approached the blackout from a structural perspective. Not participating in the Spanish political debate, leading international outlets framed the voltage collapse as a systemic failure of the European grid architecture and a warning for systems with high penetration of power electronics.

- Reuters provided the analysis with the greatest technical rigor, highlighting the role of interconnections and the voltage support deficit in the moments before the collapse.
- Le Monde adopted a descriptive approach to the cross-border impact.
- BBC focused its coverage on critical infrastructure security, documenting the safe shutdown of nuclear reactors and refuting cyberattack hypotheses.
- Financial Times headlined "Spain and Portugal blackout blamed on solar power dependency," citing experts who attributed the instability to insufficient "firm power." This argument does not correspond to the technical consensus: the system had sufficient operating reserves and four coupled nuclear reactors. The event was not an active power collapse due to a lack of firm generation but a capacitive instability phenomenon.
- The Telegraph suggested that the Spanish government was conducting an "experiment" with the electrical system. This claim was denied by Euronews Verify and Reuters Fact Check and found no support in any of the four technical reports.

### Synthesis: Asymmetric Selection of Evidence

The conclusion of the communicative analysis is that the incident was not predominantly covered as a novel technical phenomenon but was immediately framed within the pre-existing interpretive frameworks of each editorial bloc:

- Media critical of institutional management emphasized the deficit of synchronous generation and energy policy as causes, frequently omitting the impact of the meshing.
- Media favorable to the official narrative emphasized the operational error and the deficit of interconnections, frequently omitting the actual behavior of the generation fleet.

Neither bloc offered a representation of the technical multi-causality as described in the expert reports. This dynamic has implications that extend beyond media analysis: the regulatory reforms identified as necessary — updating P.O. 7.4, deploying synchronous compensators, transitioning to grid-forming mode — require sustained political consensus. The polarization of public debate around mutually exclusive causal narratives hinders the formation of that consensus.

## Social Media Reaction: Three Phases of Collective Uncertainty Management

Analysis of activity on platform X during the blackout reveals a sequential evolution in three distinct phases.

### Phase 1 (0–6 hours): Acute Uncertainty and Disinformation

The absence of government communication during the first five hours generated a *vacuum filling* process: the information vacuum was occupied by unverified sources. The high technical opacity of the overvoltage collapse favored the spread of hypotheses of sabotage, cyberattacks (the so-called "Operation Matryoshka"), and atmospheric phenomena, all of which were assimilable by the general public due to their externalizing narrative.

Superimposed on this disinformation ecosystem, a citizen response emerged consistent with *emergent norm theory*: the development of collective coping mechanisms in the face of disruption. Costumbrist humor, irony, and pragmatic normalization functioned as collective emotional regulators in the face of an unprecedented disruption.

### Phase 2 (6–72 hours): Politicization and Outrage Communication

From the official appearance onwards, digital discourse migrated towards the attribution of responsibility. This process responds to what crisis communication literature calls *outrage communication*: messages of indignation generate greater algorithmic dissemination capacity than technical explanations.

In the digital space critical of the Government, the blackout was framed as evidence of deficient management, with demands for resignations and accountability. In the space favorable to the institutional narrative, the argumentative line oriented towards positive assessment of the emergency response and defense of the energy model.

### Phase 3 (>72 hours): Late Correction with Reduced Reach

Verified technical explanations arrived with significantly less reach than the initial messages. This phenomenon responds to a structural asymmetry documented in the literature: the temporal incompatibility between the speed of viral propagation and the inevitable slowness of technical forensic investigation.

### Synthesis: Three Structural Asymmetries

The analysis identifies three structural asymmetries of the digital ecosystem in the face of highly complex technical incidents:

1. Temporal Asymmetry: The speed of viral propagation is incompatible with the pace of technical forensic investigation. The public receives hypotheses first and corrections later, with reduced reach.
2. Vacuum Asymmetry: The institutional information vacuum is not neutral — it is filled by premature causal attributions that structure the subsequent debate.
3. Algorithmic Asymmetry: Messages of political indignation have greater dissemination capacity than technical communication, regardless of their analytical accuracy.

These three asymmetries confirm that, in the face of highly complex electrical disruptions, public understanding is conditioned by the pre-existing interpretive frameworks of each communicative agent, regardless of the available technical evidence. Considering this factor in the design of crisis communication protocols for critical system operators constitutes one of the operational lessons of April 28.
