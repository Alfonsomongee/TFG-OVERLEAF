# Impacto Comunicativo

The 28 April blackout generated a second, parallel crisis alongside the electrical one: a communicative crisis whose consequences for public understanding of the incident persist well beyond supply restoration. This chapter analyses how media and social networks processed a technically complex phenomenon, and what gap opened between the expert reports' technical consensus and the narrative that reached the Spanish public.

## Institutional communication failure and the information vacuum

The voltage zero occurred at **12:33 CEST**. The first official public appearance by the head of government did not take place until **18:00 CEST** — more than **five hours later**. During that interval, a hyperconnected population, suddenly deprived of electricity and digital access, processed the emergency in the absence of any institutional interpretive framework.

This vacuum was immediately filled by what the specialist literature terms <GlossaryLink term="Crisis communication failure">crisis communication failure</GlossaryLink>: the inability of institutions to occupy the information space with verifiable technical messages during the critical post-incident window. The consequence was an episode of <GlossaryLink term="Infodemia">infodemia</GlossaryLink>: sabotage hypotheses, Russian cyberattack theories, anomalous atmospheric phenomena and governmental experiments with the power system circulated globally before system operators issued any preliminary diagnosis.

The nature of the incident made it especially vulnerable to this dynamic: a voltage collapse whose mechanics require knowledge of <GlossaryLink term="Estabilidad de tensión">voltage stability</GlossaryLink>, <GlossaryLink term="IBR">IBR</GlossaryLink> behaviour and <GlossaryLink term="OLTC (On-Load Tap Changer)">OLTC</GlossaryLink> dynamics is, by definition, **inversely proportional in accessibility** to the alternative explanations that replaced it in the public sphere.

The direct consequence was the incorporation of the incident into the political debate on the energy transition **before** any verified technical diagnosis existed. The Spanish public received for weeks causal explanations — «renewables caused the blackout», «there were not enough nuclear plants», «Europe abandoned Spain» — that the four technical reports **do not unanimously support**. The distance between the technical consensus and the dominant media narrative is itself one of the consequences of 28 April 2025, with direct implications for society's ability to evaluate the regulatory reforms the incident demonstrates are necessary.

## Press analysis: frames and technical biases

### Media critical of institutional management

Outlets such as El Mundo, ABC and La Razón articulated a narrative linking the collapse to the government's energy policy, interpreting the blackout as the foreseeable consequence of an energy transition that had prioritised intermittent sources without ensuring sufficient backup mechanisms.

Several arguments contain **verifiable inconsistencies** with the technical reports:

- **ABC** claimed the network was on the brink of collapse due to the «alarming lack of rotational inertia caused by shut-down nuclear plants». This directly contradicts the unanimous technical consensus of all four reports: official records certify that system <GlossaryLink term="Inercia (H)">inertia</GlossaryLink> was $H = 2.3$ s at the
  moment of collapse, above the ENTSO-E recommended threshold. The event was not
  an inertia-deficit collapse but a capacitive instability phenomenon.

- **El Mundo** ran «Flamanville 3 and France's 56 nuclear plants that saved anti-nuclear Sánchez during the great blackout», implicitly attributing supply restoration to nuclear technology. France's role in restoration was indeed significant — RTE activated up to 4,500 MW of support — but recovery was also underpinned by hydroelectric black start and Moroccan support, with nuclear generation as one of several factors.

- **La Razón** cited a preliminary report concluding that «the scheduling of insufficient synchronous generation with dynamic voltage control was the cause of the blackout». This finds **partial support** in the IIT-ICAI expert report: the reactive power absorption deficit in the southern zone was a documented factor. However, the same report notes that this deficit occurred in the context of the System Operator's meshing manoeuvres — a factor the coverage did not incorporate.

### Media favourable to the institutional narrative

Outlets aligned with the official narrative shifted focus toward REE's operational management, transmission network deficiencies and inherited policy. The organising premise was that the collapse was not a decarbonisation failure but the result of infrastructure not modernised at the required pace.

Some arguments are **technically consistent** with the reports:

- **El País** highlighted that exchange capacity with France stands at 3%, far below the EU 15% target. This argument is technically sound: greater interconnection capacity would have reduced the probability and severity of inter-area oscillations.

- **elDiario.es** noted that REE operated with reduced security margins despite forecasts of high solar irradiance and low synchronous generation. This is consistent with the reactive power deficit diagnosis in the south, though the coverage did not articulate the underlying Q-V curve saturation mechanism.

Deviations are also identified:

- **La Vanguardia** characterised the transpyrenean line opening as an «egotistical» act by continental mechanisms to safeguard French supply. This contradicts <GlossaryLink term="ENTSO-E">ENTSO-E</GlossaryLink>'s assessment: the 12:33:21
  CEST opening was the **automatic** actuation of Out-of-Step (OST)
  loss-of-synchronism protections in response to severe angular divergence.
  Attributing deliberate intent to an automatic protection mechanism confuses
  system protection operation with conscious operational decision-making.

- **infoLibre** published that private operators «deliberately» failed to absorb excess voltage. This incorporates the Government's «technical indiscipline» accusation but omits chronologically the first factor: the System Operator's meshing manoeuvre that injected capacitive reactive power into the network before any trip occurred.

### International coverage: structural European framing

International press approached the blackout from a structural perspective. Not participating in the Spanish political debate, major international outlets framed the voltage zero as a systemic failure of European grid architecture and a warning for systems with high power electronics penetration.

![Sample international media coverage of the blackout](/figuras/collage_internacional.png) _International coverage of the incident. Outside the Spanish political debate, international media tended toward a structural European frame, with greater emphasis on critical infrastructure security arguments. Source: own compilation._

- **Reuters** provided the most technically rigorous analysis, highlighting interconnection capacity and the voltage support deficit from conventional plants in the moments before collapse.
- **Le Monde** took a descriptive approach to the cross-border impact.
- **BBC** focused on critical infrastructure security, documenting the safe shutdown of nuclear reactors and **refuting cyberattack hypotheses**.
- **Financial Times** ran «Spain and Portugal blackout blamed on solar power dependency», citing experts attributing instability to insufficient _«firm power»_. This does not match the technical consensus: the system had sufficient operational reserves and four coupled nuclear reactors. The event was not an active power collapse from generation shortage but a capacitive voltage instability phenomenon.
- **The Telegraph** suggested the Spanish government was conducting an «experiment» with the power system. This was refuted by Euronews Verify and Reuters Fact Check and found no support in any of the four technical reports.

### Synthesis: asymmetric evidence selection

The communication analysis concludes that the incident was largely not covered as a novel technical phenomenon but immediately framed within each outlet's pre-existing editorial interpretive framework:

- **Media critical of institutional management** emphasised synchronous generation deficit and energy policy as causes, frequently omitting the impact of the meshing manoeuvre.
- **Media favourable to the official narrative** emphasised operational error and interconnection deficits, frequently omitting actual generation fleet behaviour.

Neither bloc offered a representation of the technical multicausality as described in the expert reports. This dynamic carries implications beyond media analysis: the regulatory reforms identified as necessary —P.O. 7.4 update, deployment of <GlossaryLink term="Compensadores Síncronos (SynCons)">synchronous condensers</GlossaryLink>, transition to <GlossaryLink term="GFM (Grid-Forming)">grid-forming</GlossaryLink> mode — require sustained political consensus. Polarisation of the public debate around mutually exclusive causal narratives impedes the formation of that consensus.

## Social media response: three phases of collective uncertainty management

Analysis of activity on platform X during the blackout reveals a sequential evolution across three distinct phases.

### Phase 1 (0–6 hours): acute uncertainty and disinformation

The absence of government communication for the first five hours generated a process of <GlossaryLink term="Vacuum filling (Relleno del vacío informativo)">vacuum filling</GlossaryLink>: the information void was occupied by unverified sources. The high technical opacity of the overvoltage collapse favoured the propagation of sabotage hypotheses, cyberattack theories (the so-called «Operation Matryoshka») and atmospheric phenomena — all easily assimilated by the general public due to their externalising narratives.

Overlaid on this disinformation ecosystem, a citizen response emerged consistent with <GlossaryLink term="Emergent norm theory">emergent norm theory</GlossaryLink>: the development of collective coping mechanisms in the face of disruption. Everyday humour, irony and pragmatic normalisation functioned as collective emotional regulators during an unprecedented disruption.

Three response patterns are identifiable: pragmatic normalisation and humour as a coping mechanism; institutional criticism through viral formats; and active search for causal explanations in the absence of official information. ### Phase 2 (6–72 hours): politicisation and outrage communication

Following the official statement, digital discourse migrated toward responsibility attribution. This process corresponds to what crisis communication literature terms <GlossaryLink term="Outrage communication (Comunicación de indignación)">outrage communication</GlossaryLink>: indignation messages generate greater algorithmic diffusion capacity than technical explanations.

In the digital space critical of the government, the blackout was framed as evidence of poor management, with demands for resignations and accountability. In the space favourable to the institutional narrative, the argumentative line focused on positively evaluating the emergency response and defending the current energy model.

![Reactions from political figures on X during the 72 hours following the blackout](/figuras/collage_politicos.png) *Publications by Spanish political leaders on X during the 72 hours following the incident. None of the messages analysed addressed technical aspects of the overvoltage collapse, the <GlossaryLink term="Tap-Lag">Tap-Lag</GlossaryLink> phenomenon or the role of the meshing manoeuvre. Political communication from both positions was oriented towards responsibility attribution without incorporating the technical elements of the expert diagnosis. Source: X screenshots, own compilation.*

### Phase 3 (>72 hours): delayed correction with reduced reach

Verified technical explanations arrived with significantly lower reach than the initial messages. This reflects a structurally documented asymmetry: the temporal incompatibility between viral propagation speed and the inevitable slowness of technical forensic investigation.

### Synthesis: three structural asymmetries

The analysis identifies three structural asymmetries in the digital ecosystem when facing high technical complexity incidents:

1. **Temporal asymmetry**: viral propagation speed is incompatible with the pace of technical forensic investigation. The public receives hypotheses first and corrections later, with reduced reach.
2. **Vacuum asymmetry**: institutional information voids are not neutral — they are occupied by premature causal attributions that structure the subsequent debate.
3. **Algorithmic asymmetry**: political indignation messages have greater diffusion capacity than technical communication, regardless of their analytical precision.

These three asymmetries confirm that, in the face of technically complex electrical disruptions, public understanding is conditioned by each communicative agent's pre-existing interpretive frameworks, **regardless of the available technical evidence**. The consideration of this factor in the design of crisis communication protocols for critical systems operators constitutes one of the operational lessons of 28 April 2025.
