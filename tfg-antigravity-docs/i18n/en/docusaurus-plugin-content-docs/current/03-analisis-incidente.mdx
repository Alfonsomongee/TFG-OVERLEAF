<GlitchTitle>Incident Analysis</GlitchTitle>

The voltage collapse of 28 April was not an instantaneous event: it was the culmination of a progressive, measurable degradation of the network's static and dynamic stability. The forensic reconstruction of the four collapse phases — from the morning oscillations to the definitive isolation of the peninsula at 12:33:29 CEST — allows precise identification of the physical mechanisms that no conventional protection automation could arrest.

## Phase 0: the weakened network and the meshing manoeuvre

On the morning of 28 April, the system was operating on an extremely weak topology. With demand at minimum levels, **35.8% of the 400 kV network in the southern zone** and **34.3% in the central zone** remained disconnected, creating a high transfer impedance scenario. Against this background, system dynamics manifested as <GlossaryLink term="Oscilaciones electromecánicas">electromechanical oscillations</GlossaryLink> in two distinct modes:

- **12:03 CEST** — Forced oscillation at **0.6 Hz**, amplitude 70 mHz, taking 4 minutes and 42 seconds to damp out.
- **12:19 CEST** — Inter-area mode at **0.2 Hz** (East-Centre-West): the Iberian Peninsula began oscillating in counter-phase with respect to the continent, stressing the Pyrenean interconnection links.

During both transients, the <GlossaryLink term="Damping ratio">damping ratio</GlossaryLink> collapsed to values near **1%**, well below the minimum **5%** threshold required by P.O. 13.1. This behaviour was not unprecedented: analogous episodes on 16, 22 and 24 April had been treated as isolated incidents rather than symptoms of systemic degradation.

> ![Voltages recorded at Núñez de Balboa 400 kV during precursor events and 28 April](/figuras/nunez_balboa_precursores.png) _Figure 12. Voltages at Núñez de Balboa (400 kV) during the 22, 24 and 28 April precursor events. The succession of overvoltage peaks evidences the progressive narrowing of <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power</GlossaryLink> control margins. Source: IIT-ICAI._

To contain these oscillations, REE executed a <GlossaryLink term="Mallado">meshing</GlossaryLink> manoeuvre: between 12:03 and 12:30 CEST it reconnected **11 400 kV lines** that had been open due to low demand. The manoeuvre achieved its immediate objective — reducing impedance and arresting the oscillation whip — but introduced, according to the IIT-ICAI expert report, a significant side effect: through the <GlossaryLink term="Efecto Ferranti">Ferranti Effect</GlossaryLink>, the no-load reconnected lines abruptly injected capacitive reactive power into a network that already lacked absorption margin. Quantification of this injection is one of the central axes of the technical dispute: the ICAI report estimates up to **2.4 GVAr**; the NREL's independent analysis adopts a more conservative estimate of **1,050 MVAr**. Both figures agree that the injection exceeded the system's residual absorption capacity, estimated at that moment at barely **3.3 GVAr** against the usual 5.8 GVAr.

IIT-ICAI's expert simulations estimate that after the meshing manoeuvre, the distance to the collapse point at the Carmona 400 kV bus contracted from **2,964 MW** to **1,268 MW** — a **57% reduction** bringing the system to the threshold of capacitive saturation.

## Phase 1: loss of damping and root trip (12:32:57 CEST)

The nature of the first transient — the 0.6 Hz oscillation at 12:03 CEST — is the subject of direct technical controversy. REE classifies it as a forced oscillation originating in the control loop of a photovoltaic plant in Badajoz. The ICAI report argues, conversely, that it was a natural inter-area mode exacerbated by a systemic damping ratio of **1%**, which it links to the absence of Power System Stabilisers (PSS) in the Andalusian combined-cycle units that were offline.

The instability intensified at **12:19 CEST** with the **0.2 Hz** mode, confirmed by European PMU telemetry as the natural East-Centre-West mode of the continental synchronous area: the Iberian Peninsula's reduced rotating mass was oscillating in counter-phase with the European inertia centre, materialising the whip effect described in Chapter 2.

The point of no return was reached at **12:32:57 CEST**. A 400/220 kV transformer at a collector substation in the province of **Granada** disconnected through overvoltage protection activation, having detected approximately **244 kV** on the 220 kV secondary — exceeding the equipment's dielectric safety threshold. The critical impact was not the 355 MW of <GlossaryLink term="Potencia activa">active power</GlossaryLink> lost — a manageable figure — but the instantaneous, irrecoverable loss of **165 MVAr** of inductive reactive power absorption capacity. In a system saturated with capacitive reactive power and operating with reduced <GlossaryLink term="Colapso Q-V">Q-V</GlossaryLink> margins, the disappearance of this sink caused a step increase in voltage at surrounding transmission nodes: the physical trigger of the chain reaction.

## Phase 2: the Tap-Lag phenomenon and voltage decoupling (12:32:57–12:33:18 CEST)

The 21 seconds following the root trip constitute the technical core of the forensic debate. After the Granada disconnection, the rising voltage trend on the transmission network revealed a divergence between the behaviour of the 400 kV infrastructure and the underlying collector networks: the <GlossaryLink term="Tap-Lag">Tap-Lag</GlossaryLink> phenomenon.

During Phase 1, voltage fluctuations caused by frequency oscillations had led 400/220 kV and 400/132 kV transformers to adjust their <GlossaryLink term="OLTC (On-Load Tap Changer)">OLTCs</GlossaryLink>, raising tap positions to boost secondary voltages. When the overvoltage surge hit the 400 kV network, these transformers were trapped by their own mechanical inertia: OLTC motor and gear mechanisms are intentionally slowed by time delays to prevent mechanical hunting under transient disturbances. Faced with an ultra-fast electrical transient, the tap changers proved too slow to reverse their transformation ratios in time.

> ![Oscillographic evidence of Tap-Lag voltage decoupling between 400 kV primary and collector secondary](/figuras/tap_lag_decoupling.png) _Figure 15. Voltage decoupling between the 400 kV primary and the collector secondary during Phase 2. While the voltage increase on the 400 kV side remained moderate, the OLTC inertia amplified the transient on the collector side. Source: ENTSO-E._

This mechanical failure created an **observability blindspot in REE's control room**. The <GlossaryLink term="SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition)">SCADA</GlossaryLink> system monitored the 400 kV primary, showing high but apparently compliant voltages below the exceptional normative limit of **435 kV** (P.O. 1.1). However, downstream, in the 220 kV and 132 kV networks, voltages climbed above **1.2 p.u.** — thresholds invisible to the national dispatch centre due to its limited observability of the underlying network.

> ![Overvoltage propagation heatmap across the transmission network during Phase 2](/figuras/heatmap_propagation.png) _Figure 16. Propagation of overvoltages across the 400 kV network during Phase 2 (12:32:00–12:33:18 CEST). Source: Government Analysis Committee / REE._

Faced with voltages exceeding their power electronics tolerances, solar and wind plant inverters triggered their internal overvoltage protection systems (ANSI function 59). This is where one of the incident's most consequential forensic disagreements arises: REE classified these losses as **«inadequate trips»**, arguing that no disconnection thresholds were exceeded on its 400 kV network; the ICAI analysis contends that the plants **acted correctly and in conformity with regulations** given the actual voltages experienced at their connection points, downstream of the OLTC transformers. The Tap-Lag phenomenon thereby created the conditions for a positive-feedback disconnection cascade.

## Phase 3: The Path to Voltage Zero (12:33:18—12:33:29 CEST)

In 11 seconds, the peninsular grid destroyed itself through a vicious cycle dictated by power flow equations. As IBR plants mass-disconnected to self-protect, the network instantly lost its capacity to absorb reactive power. Simultaneously, the falling active power reduced flow in the 400 kV lines, increasing their capacitive injection through the Ferranti Effect and driving voltages above **443 kV** at the most stressed nodes. Every disconnection raised voltage, forcing further disconnections.

> ![Spatial propagation of the generation loss cascade during Phase 3](/figuras/cascada_desconexiones.png) _Figure 17. Geographic propagation of the cascade during the eleven seconds of Phase 3. Source: Government Analysis Committee._

The most severe losses were concentrated initially in **Badajoz** (over **725 MW** lost at 12:33:16 CEST) and, one second later, at the **Segovia, Seville and Huelva** substations (930 MW additional at 12:33:17 CEST). In the five-second window between 12:33:19 and 12:33:24 CEST, the cascade disconnected more than **15 GW of generation** — nearly **60% of instantaneous national demand** — taking the system beyond any possibility of recovery.

As the cascade destroyed remaining generation, frequency collapsed with extreme <GlossaryLink term="RoCoF (Rate of Change of Frequency)">RoCoF</GlossaryLink>, activating <GlossaryLink term="UFLS (Underfrequency Load Shedding)">UFLS</GlossaryLink> schemes. At the **49.5 Hz** threshold crossing at 12:33:20 CEST, over **2,000 MW** of hydraulic pumping was automatically shed, followed by multiple industrial demand steps.

> ![Coupled evolution of voltage and frequency during Phase 3](/figuras/tension_frecuencia_colapso.png) _Figure 18. Coupled evolution of voltage (kV) and frequency (Hz) during Phase 3. The voltage increase above 1.10 p.u. precedes the frequency collapse in time, confirming the incident as a primarily capacitive, not inertial, collapse. Source: Government Analysis Committee._

The UFLS activation revealed what analysts have termed the **UFLS paradox**: by shedding active load to stabilise frequency, the scheme simultaneously eliminated the inductive reactive power consumption of that same demand. In a network saturated with capacitive reactive power, removing the last inductive sinks — the disconnected motors and industrial loads — produced a renewed overvoltage surge, **reinforcing the very collapse the UFLS was designed to arrest**. Protection logic designed for frequency deficit events proved counterproductive against a collapse driven primarily by voltage instability.

In the final moments, the massive internal generation deficit triggered a violent reversal of Pyrenean flows: the AC lines attempted to import up to **4,609 MW** of emergency power from France. Simultaneously, HVDC link INELFE-1 — locked in PMODE1 since 12:08 CEST — continued extracting **1,000 MW** from the Iberian system, reducing net support capacity from France by **22%**.

> ![France interconnection exchange and loss of synchronism during Phase 3](/figuras/interconexion_francia_colapso.png) _Figure 19. Pyrenean flow reversal during Phase 3: emergency import of up to 4,609 MW via AC lines, simultaneous 1,000 MW HVDC extraction in PMODE1, and AC line trip on loss of synchronism at 12:33:21 CEST. Source: Government Analysis Committee / REE._

At **12:33:21 CEST**, loss-of- <GlossaryLink term="Sincronismo">synchronism</GlossaryLink> protections opened the AC interconnection links to prevent propagation to the continental European system, fully isolating the Iberian Peninsula. The definitive **voltage zero** was confirmed at **12:33:29.741 CEST** with the trip of the last generating unit — closing the most severe systemic incident documented in the history of the continental European power system.
