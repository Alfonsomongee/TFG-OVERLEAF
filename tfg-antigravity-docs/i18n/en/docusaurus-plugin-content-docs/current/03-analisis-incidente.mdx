<GlitchTitle>Incident Analysis</GlitchTitle>

The voltage collapse of April 28 was not an instantaneous event: it was the culmination of a progressive and measurable degradation of the grid's static and dynamic stability. The forensic reconstruction of the four phases of the collapse —from the morning oscillations to the definitive isolation of the peninsula at 12:33:29 CEST— allows for the precise identification of the physical mechanisms that no conventional protection automation could stop. **Here, in all its magnitude, the technical vertex of the Energy Trilemma manifests itself: the physical vulnerability of a system dominated by inverters and devoid of traditional inertia.**

## Phase 0: the weakened grid and the meshing maneuver {#fase-0}

<span id="t1"></span>
On the morning of April 28, the system operated on an extremely weak topology. With demand at minimums, 35.8% of the 400 kV network in the southern zone and 34.3% in the central zone remained disconnected, configuring a high transfer impedance scenario. On this network, the system dynamics manifested as electromechanical oscillations with two distinct modes:

- 12:03 CEST — Forced oscillation of 0.6 Hz, amplitude 70 mHz, which took 4 minutes and 42 seconds to dampen.
- 12:19 CEST — Inter-area mode of 0.2 Hz East-Center-West: the Iberian Peninsula began to oscillate in counterphase relative to the continent, straining the Pyrenean links.

During both transients, the damping ratio plummeted to values near 1%, well below the minimum 5% threshold required by technical literature (Kundur, 1994). This behavior was not unprecedented: analogous episodes on April 16, 22, and 24 had been treated as isolated incidents rather than symptoms of systemic degradation.

<KeyFact id="mallado-ferranti">

To contain these oscillations, REE executed a meshing maneuver: between 12:03 and 12:30 CEST, it reconnected 11 400 kV lines that had remained open due to low demand. The maneuver achieved its immediate objective —reducing impedance and halting the whiplash of oscillations— but introduced, according to the IIT-ICAI report, a relevant side effect: due to the Ferranti Effect, the re-energized lines abruptly injected capacitive reactive power into a grid that already lacked absorption margin. The quantification of this injection is one of the axes of the technical dispute: the ICAI report estimates it at up to 2.4 GVAr; the independent NREL analysis adopts a more conservative estimate of 1,050 MVAr. Both figures agree that the injection exceeded the system's residual absorption capacity, estimated at that time at barely 3.3 GVAr compared to the usual 5.8 GVAr (see [Annex IX · Methodology and Models](./anexo-metodologia-modelos-datos-vivos), dedicated to methodology, models, and simulators).

</KeyFact>

## Phase 1: loss of damping and root trip (12:32:57 CEST) {#fase-1}

The nature of the first transient —the 0.6 Hz oscillation at 12:03 CEST— is a subject of direct technical controversy. REE classifies it as a forced oscillation originating in the control loop of a photovoltaic plant in Badajoz. The ICAI report argues, instead, that it was a natural inter-area mode exacerbated by a systemic damping ratio of 1%, whose origin it links to the absence of Power System Stabilizers (PSS) in the Andalusian combined cycle plants that remained offline.

<FrequencyChart isGallery={true} />

_Figure C4. Frequency evolution during the terminal phase of the collapse. Data: ENTSO-E / REE. Interactive visualization of own elaboration._

The evolution of frequency should not be interpreted as the primary cause of the blackout, but as the terminal manifestation of an instability that was already developing in the voltage and reactive power plane. The frequency drop appears when the cascade of disconnections has already destroyed a substantial fraction of the available generation. Therefore, frequency chronologically orders the final phase of the event, but does not exhaust its causal mechanism: the physical origin lies in capacitive saturation, the narrowing of the Q-V margin, and the progressive loss of reactive absorption capacity.

The instability intensified at 12:19 CEST with the 0.2 Hz mode, confirmed by European phasor telemetry as the natural East-Center-West mode of the synchronous area: the reduced Iberian rotating mass oscillated in counterphase relative to the European center of inertia, materializing the whip effect described in Chapter 2.

<span id="t5"></span>
<KeyFact id="granada-disparo">

The point of no return was reached at 12:32:57 CEST. A 400/220 kV transformer in a collector substation in the province of Granada disconnected due to the activation of its overvoltage protections, upon detecting a value of approximately *244 kV* on the 220 kV secondary, exceeding the dielectric safety threshold. The magnitude of the impact did not lie in the 355 MW of lost active power —a manageable figure for the system— but in the instantaneous and irrecoverable loss of 165 MVAr of inductive reactive power absorption capacity. In a system saturated with capacitive reactive power and with reduced Q-V margins, the disappearance of this sink caused an upward voltage step in the surrounding nodes: the physical trigger of the chain reaction (see [Annex III · Protections and Disconnections](./anexo-cascada-protecciones-desconexiones)).

</KeyFact>

## Phase 2: the Tap-Lag phenomenon and voltage decoupling (12:32:57–12:33:18 CEST) {#fase-2-taplag}

The 21 seconds following the root trip are the technical core of the forensic debate.

<span id="t4"></span>
After the Granada disconnection, the increasing voltage trend in the transmission network revealed a divergence between the behavior of the 400 kV infrastructure and the underlying collector networks: the Tap-Lag phenomenon.

During Phase 1, voltage fluctuations and dips caused by frequency oscillations had led the 400/220 kV and 400/132 kV transformers to adjust their OLTCs by raising taps to increase voltage on the secondaries. When the overvoltage erupted on the 400 kV network, these transformers became trapped in their own mechanical inertia: the motor and gear mechanisms of the OLTCs are intentionally slowed down by time delays to avoid mechanical oscillations during fleeting disturbances. Faced with an ultra-fast electrical transient, the tap changers proved excessively slow and could not lower the transformation ratio in time.

Tap-Lag turns a partially observable overvoltage into an invisible systemic threat. The transmission network could still appear within acceptable limits while downstream collector networks already exceeded thresholds incompatible with the continued connection of inverters. This divergence explains why plant protections could act correctly from their connection point and, at the same time, appear premature from the operator's aggregate perspective. The phenomenon was not a local detail: it was the physical bridge between the prior meshing and the cascade of ANSI 59 trips.

Faced with voltages unsustainable for their power electronics, the inverters of solar and wind plants activated their internal overvoltage protection systems (ANSI function 59). Here lies one of the greatest forensic discrepancies of the incident: REE classified these losses as "inadequate trips" arguing that no disconnection values existed on its 400 kV network; the ICAI analysis maintains that the plants acted correctly in response to the actual overvoltage experienced at their connection points, downstream of the OLTC transformer. Tap-Lag thus configured the conditions for a positive feedback loop of cascading disconnections.

## Phase 3: the path to voltage zero (12:33:18—12:33:29 CEST) {#fase-3}

In 11 seconds, the peninsular network destroyed itself through a vicious circle dictated by the power flow equations. As IBR plants massively disconnected to protect themselves, the network instantly lost its ability to absorb reactive power. Simultaneously, the drop in active power reduced the flow on the 400 kV lines, increasing their capacitive injection due to the Ferranti effect and pushing the voltage above 443 kV in the most stressed nodes. Each disconnection raised the voltage, forcing further disconnections.

<span id="t6"></span>
The most severe blows of the cascade initially concentrated in Badajoz (at 12:33:16 CEST, *t=19 s*) and, in the immediately following second, in the substations of Segovia, Seville, and Huelva. In the window of the critical five seconds (12:33:19–12:33:24 CEST), the chain reaction disconnected <CuestionAbierta metricKey="potencias_cascada.perdida_generacion_total_cascada">15,000 MW</CuestionAbierta> of generation (more than 50% of the prior instantaneous Iberian capacity), leaving <CuestionAbierta metricKey="potencias_cascada.demanda_sin_suministro_espana">25.2 GW</CuestionAbierta> of Spanish demand completely without supply and driving the system beyond any possibility of recovery. (For the exhaustive second-by-second chronology and the power volumes of each disconnection, see [Annex III · Protections and Disconnections](./anexo-cascada-protecciones-desconexiones), where the detailed documentation of protections, disconnections, and cascade propagation is preserved).

As the cascade destroyed the remaining generation, the frequency began to plummet with an extreme RoCoF (with estimated secondary peaks of <CuestionAbierta metricKey="rocof.rocof_pico">1.5 Hz/s</CuestionAbierta>), activating the UFLS schemes. Upon crossing the 49.5 Hz threshold at 12:33:20 CEST, more than 2,000 MW of hydraulic pumping were automatically disconnected, and as the network fell below <CuestionAbierta metricKey="cronologia_cascada.frecuencia_umbral_deslastre">49.0 Hz</CuestionAbierta>, the first steps of industrial demand were shed.

<KeyFact id="paradoja-ufls">

The activation of UFLS revealed the so-called UFLS paradox: by cutting active consumption to stabilize frequency, the scheme simultaneously eliminated the inductive reactive power consumption of that same demand. In a network saturated with capacitive reactive power, suppressing the last sinks —motors and industrial loads— produced a new surge of overvoltages, reinforcing the collapse that the UFLS itself was intended to halt. Defense logics designed for frequency deficits proved counterproductive against a collapse driven primarily by voltage instability.

</KeyFact>

In the final moments, the massive deficit of internal generation caused a violent reversal of Pyrenean flows: the AC lines reached a transient import peak of approximately 3,800 MW of active power (ENTSO-E Factual, pp.108-109) from France. Simultaneously, the HVDC INELFE-1 link —fixed in PMODE1 since 12:08 CEST— continued extracting on the order of 1 GW of active power from the Iberian system (the precise breakdown by circuit and capacity can be audited in [Annex IV · Interconnections and Flows](./anexo-interconexiones-flujos)). This asymmetric operation drastically contracted the net support capacity from the continent.
