<GlitchTitle>Analysis of Official Reports</GlitchTitle>

The cross-analysis of the four reports on the 28 April blackout reveals an architecture of disputes that goes well beyond technical disagreement: three institutionally opposed narratives of the same sequence of events, with direct legal, economic and regulatory implications. This chapter examines the internal coherence of each narrative, their verified points of consensus, and their irreconcilable contradictions.

## The Government and REE position: multifactorial causality and regulatory non-compliance

The official narrative classifies the blackout not as an operational design error or a direct consequence of dispatch manoeuvres, but as a **multifactorial event**: the convergence of valley demand, massive <GlossaryLink term="IBR">IBR</GlossaryLink> penetration and inadequate technical behaviour by private agents that, acting simultaneously, exceeded the thresholds of the <GlossaryLink term="Criterio N-1">*N*−1 Security Criterion</GlossaryLink>.

| Factor | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Insufficient voltage control** | Non-compliance with P.O. 7.4 by synchronous generators and RCR installations |
| **Electromechanical oscillations** | Conditioning factors for the pre-collapse system state |
| **Generation disconnections** | Some classified as premature → final vector of the overvoltage cascade |

_Multifactorial causality scheme per the Government Analysis Committee._

REE's technical defence of the <GlossaryLink term="Mallado">meshing</GlossaryLink> manoeuvre merits careful examination. The report highlights an aspect frequently omitted from criticism: the initial oscillations produced not overvoltages but **voltage drops** at transmission network nodes. Against this risk, the Control Centre executed strictly protocolised actions — circuit reconnection, shunt reactor coupling, HVDC coordination to switch to PMODE1 — acknowledging that these manoeuvres «contributed to the voltage rise» but classifying them as necessary. REE argues that any subsequent voltage increase would have been absorbable had agents complied with their regulatory obligations.

![Thermal voltage maps of the 400 kV network in the moments preceding the root trip](/figuras/mapas_termicos_tension_ree.png) _Voltage mapping of the 400 kV network (12:30–12:32:57 CEST) per the System Operator's analysis. REE maintains that voltage profiles remained within P.O. 1.1 operating ranges until the onset of disconnections in the underlying collector networks. Source: Red Eléctrica._

The central argument transfers responsibility to the generation fleet: analysis of the 850 highest-generating installations documents that approximately **22%** did not meet the power factor criterion under RD 413/2014, though the report itself notes this may be partly attributable to the capacitive effect of evacuation infrastructure under low generation. A thermal plant in the southern zone not only failed to absorb inductive <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power</GlossaryLink>, but injected additional capacitive reactive power into the system. For REE, the collective inhibition of the private fleet deprived the system of the dynamic margin needed to contain the transient.

On inertia, REE explicitly rules it out as the material cause: the system operated with $H = 2.3$ s, above the 2 s threshold recommended by <GlossaryLink term="ENTSO-E">ENTSO-E</GlossaryLink>, and «the overvoltage wave would have triggered the cascade effect in any case», since overvoltage conditions persisted until 12:33:23 CEST independently of frequency.

The government narrative shows appreciable internal coherence and identifies documented regulatory breaches. However, it does not address two key arguments from the ICAI expert report: the magnitude of the capacitive reactive power injected by the meshing manoeuvre, and the operator's observability limitation on the 220 kV network through the <GlossaryLink term="Tap-Lag">Tap-Lag</GlossaryLink> phenomenon — two elements which, according to ICAI, would have made the collapse inevitable regardless of generator compliance levels.

## The generator sector position (ICAI / AELEC): excessive meshing and inevitable collapse

In contrast to the government narrative, the IIT-ICAI expert report — together with Compass Lexecon and INESC TEC — formally diagnoses the event as a **voltage collapse**: a <GlossaryLink term="Estabilidad de tensión">voltage instability</GlossaryLink> phenomenon driven by the exhaustion of reactive power absorption capacity, in which REE's own preventive actions acted as the primary cascade vector.

The core of the critique is quantitative. In response to oscillations from 12:03 CEST, REE coupled **11 no-load 400 kV circuits**. Through the <GlossaryLink term="Efecto Ferranti">Ferranti Effect</GlossaryLink>, these lines behaved as large capacitor banks, injecting between **1.05 and 2.4 GVAr** of capacitive reactive power into a network already lacking absorption margin. Simulations estimate that the collapse margin at the Carmona 400 kV bus contracted from **2,964 MW** to **1,268 MW** — a **57% reduction** bringing the system to the threshold of capacitive saturation.

![Q-V curves at Carmona 400 kV bus per ICAI expert analysis](/figuras/fluctuaciones_tension_previas.png) _<GlossaryLink term="Colapso Q-V">Q-V</GlossaryLink> voltage stability curves at Carmona 400 kV. The meshing manoeuvres (LIN&SHN) displaced the operating point, contracting the collapse margin by 57%. The red dashed line marks the V = 1.2 p.u. limit (480 kV). Source: IIT-ICAI / Compass Lexecon._

The discrepancy between ICAI's (2.4 GVAr) and NREL's independent (1.05 GVAr) estimates does not alter the shared conclusion: in both cases, the capacitive injection exceeded the system's residual absorption capacity, estimated at barely **3.3 GVAr** against the usual 5.8 GVAr.

On the cascade disconnections, the generator sector refutes the «inadequate trips» classification by pointing to the operator's observability limitation. While the <GlossaryLink term="SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition)">SCADA</GlossaryLink> showed 418 kV on the 400 kV network — within normative limits — the Tap-Lag phenomenon created a blind spot in the collector networks: the mechanical inertia of the <GlossaryLink term="OLTC (On-Load Tap Changer)">OLTCs</GlossaryLink> amplified the transient to the 220 kV networks, where **244 kV** (>110% nominal voltage) was reached. The owner of the Granada substation confirmed that its protection «was correctly set and operated correctly».

![Oscillographic record of the root trip in Granada at 12:32:56.993 CEST](/figuras/aluvion_alertas_sobretension_sur.png) _Oscillogram of the root trip (Granada, 12:32:56.993 CEST). Upper panel: three-phase current stable until upstream breaker trip. Lower panel: collector secondary voltage — phase A reaches ~145 kV (>1.10 p.u.) — invisible to REE's SCADA on the 400 kV network through the Tap-Lag effect. Source: IIT-ICAI / AELEC._

The second argument line targets the regulatory framework: P.O. 7.4, in its pre-reform version, forced IBR to operate at a **fixed power factor**, preventing their power electronics from dynamically absorbing or injecting reactive power despite possessing this technological capability. This restriction excluded **82% of instantaneous generation** from active voltage control, concentrating operational responsibility on the scarce available synchronous thermal fleet.

The resulting quantitative imbalance was, per the expert report, **mathematically insurmountable**: in the southern zone, REE had barely **0.2 GVAr** of inductive reactive absorption capacity against an estimated capacitive injection exceeding **0.7 GVAr** induced by its own manoeuvres.

![Reactive power balance in the southern zone at 12:30 CEST per IIT-ICAI](/figuras/asimetria_balance_reactiva_sur.png) _Reactive power balance at 12:30 CEST. REE's manoeuvres added 0.7 GVAr capacitive on top of the existing 3.4 GVAr, while conventional synchronous generation provided barely 0.2 GVAr of absorption. Net deficit: −0.6 GVAr. Source: IIT-ICAI / Compass Lexecon._

The expert report's conclusion identifies **three factors directly attributable to the operator and regulator**: the meshing manoeuvre that saturated Q-V margins, the structural inobservability of the 220 kV network through Tap-Lag, and the regulatory framework (P.O. 7.4) that prevented 82% of the generation fleet from participating in dynamic voltage control.

## The European perspective (ENTSO-E): NC RfG inadequacy and synchronous separation

ENTSO-E's expert panel offers the most structural of the three perspectives. Its central thesis rejects attribution of the blackout to renewable penetration _per se_: the collapse resulted not from excess renewable generation or primary inertia shortage, but from the **limitations of the voltage profile control framework**. The incident reveals the asymmetry between accelerated renewable asset deployment and the partial obsolescence of the network codes governing their dynamic behaviour.

The analysis identifies a fundamental technical tension: most of the installations that disconnected possessed the power electronics needed for robust voltage control, but Spanish regulations **prohibited or did not require** this dynamic service, forcing them to operate at static power factors under RD 413/2014. This regulatory restriction deprived the system of the distributed reactive support that could have damped the capacitive injection from no-load lines.

In direct response, ENTSO-E has consolidated in its Phase II Report the update to **NC RfG 2.0**, which proposes mandating <GlossaryLink term="GFM (Grid-Forming)">grid-forming</GlossaryLink> operation for all non-synchronous modules ≥ 1 MW:

| Requirement | <GlossaryLink term="NC RfG">Current NC RfG</GlossaryLink> | NC RfG 2.0 (proposed) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operating mode | _Grid-following_ | _Grid-forming_ mandatory |
| Threshold | Varies by type/country | ≥ 1 MW (PPM and ESM) |
| Voltage control | Fixed power factor | Continuous dynamic V and Q control |
| Inertia | Not required for IBR | <GlossaryLink term="Inercia Sintética">Synthetic inertia</GlossaryLink> mandatory |
| Black Start | Not required for IBR | Island start-up capability |
| Synchronism reference | External (PLL on grid) | Internally generated (ideal voltage source) |

On the topology of the incident, ENTSO-E analyses the loss of <GlossaryLink term="Sincronismo">synchronism</GlossaryLink> at **12:33:19 CEST**: the system passed from exporting 469 MW (12:32:57) to importing a maximum of **3,807 MW** (12:33:19), at which point the angular pole slip became unsustainable and _Out-of-Step_ (OST) protections acted at **12:33:21 CEST**, ordering the opening of the transpyrenean circuits.

![Spain-France exchange evolution and loss of synchronism (12:32:57–12:33:20 CEST)](/figuras/perdida_sincronismo_frontera.png) _Active power exchange at the Spain-France border during Phase 3. Upper panel: total net flow (469 MW export → 3,807 MW maximum import). Lower panel: per-circuit breakdown. The terminal oscillation reflects pole divergence prior to definitive breaker opening. Source: ENTSO-E._

This separation prevented the capacitive collapse and frequency collapse — with a <GlossaryLink term="RoCoF (Rate of Change of Frequency)">RoCoF</GlossaryLink> exceeding **1.5 Hz/s** — from propagating to the French system and the rest of Continental Europe.

ENTSO-E's final warning is structural: coordinated security analyses (CSA) and Common Grid Models (CGM), based on static load flows and the *N*−1 Criterion, **assessed the system as «Normal»** until moments before the collapse. For ENTSO-E, this limitation indicates that continental operative security assessment standards — designed for conventional synchronous machines — are structurally insufficient to prevent collapses in systems with high power electronics penetration.

## Consensus and divergences: the technical fracture across three axes

### Verified technical consensus

Despite the polarisation of the debate, the four reports converge on a set of technical conclusions that qualify or refute the most widespread media narratives:

| Consensus point | Shared technical basis | GOV | REE | ICAI | ENTSO-E |
| --- | --- | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| **Inertia was not the root cause** | System operated at H = 2.3 s (above 2.0 s threshold). Greater inertia would have slowed the frequency collapse by fractions of a second, without preventing the overvoltage collapse. | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| **Overvoltage collapse, not frequency deficit** | The material cause was capacitive instability (reactive power Q imbalance), not active power P loss or primary frequency drop. | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| **Active power reserves were sufficient** | Upward tertiary reserve exceeded 7,000 MW. No generation capacity crisis occurred. | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| **UFLS aggravated the voltage collapse** | Load shedding eliminated the last inductive reactive sinks, accelerating the voltage escalation. | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| **Synchronism protections acted correctly** | The opening of transpyrenean circuits at 12:33:21 was technically and normatively correct. | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| **IBR regulatory framework was inadequate** | P.O. 7.4 and RD 413/2014 prevented IBR from participating in dynamic voltage control despite possessing the technological capability. | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| **Static N−1 criterion is insufficient** | Static security tools assessed the system as safe hours before the collapse, unable to anticipate ultra-fast capacitive instability dynamics. | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

## Interpretive synthesis: three governance fractures

The comparative analysis allows the inter-party dispute to be transcended in favour of a second-order diagnosis: the 28 April 2025 blackout was neither an unforeseeable «black swan» nor the failure of a specific technology, but the **convergent manifestation of three governance fractures** coexisting in the Iberian power system.

### Operational fracture: causality versus responsibility

The meshing-as-trigger thesis (ICAI) and the collective non-compliance thesis (REE / Government) are not mutually exclusive in technical terms: both factors contributed to the capacitive saturation that narrowed the Q-V margins. What they are mutually exclusive in is their legal and economic implication. This causal ambiguity is the predictable result of operating a network without sufficient dynamic instrumentation: when the system lacks PMU coverage at all critical nodes and real-time voltage stability calculation tools, subsequent forensic reconstruction is inevitably subject to interpretation. The tacit consensus among all parties — which no report states explicitly — is that the **monitoring architecture proved insufficient** both for managing the incident in real time and for resolving it unambiguously after the fact.

### Regulatory fracture: 20th-century regulation applied to a 21st-century grid

ENTSO-E's position — the most structural of the three — argues that both the operator and generators acted within the limits of a regulatory framework that was inadequate for the system it purported to govern. P.O. 7.4 and RD 413/2014 designed a voltage control scheme for a network dominated by slowly-responding synchronous masses; applying this to a grid with 82% IBR penetration is, in control engineering terms, equivalent to attempting to stabilise a millisecond-response system with a regulator designed for minute-scale dynamics. This inadequacy was not unknown: the P.O. 7.4 update proposal had been **pending regulatory approval for years**. The Government's own report acknowledges that its entry into force would have been the most relevant change to have prevented the collapse. The 28 April blackout is, in this sense, the **measurable cost of a regulatory delay**.

### Systemic fracture: static security tools in a dynamic network

The unanimous consensus on the insufficiency of the static *N*−1 Criterion points to a vulnerability that extends well beyond the Iberian case. The load flow models that evaluated the system as «Normal» are mathematically incapable of representing capacitive instability phenomena in low-inertia networks: they do not solve differential equations, do not model inverter control loop dynamics, and do not calculate Q-V margins in real time. The speed of collapse progression — from first trips to voltage zero in under **90 seconds** — places the phenomenon outside the temporal horizon of any human intervention: when the operator perceived the severity of the transient, the system was already irrecoverable. The only viable response at this timescale is **structural prevention through real-time dynamic security analysis**, whose deployment at European regional coordination centres constitutes one of the most urgent reforms identified by ENTSO-E.

### The limit of the centralised paradigm

Underlying the three fractures is a deeper tension that the reports address fragmentarily but which comparative analysis allows to be formulated clearly: the centralised operation paradigm — a single operator with visibility limited to the 400 kV level, managing voltage with discrete switched reactors and dispatching synchronous generation through hourly market signals — was designed for a network where synchronous mass provided natural damping and generators responded in seconds. In a network where **82% of generation consists of inverters that respond in milliseconds**, self- provide synchronism references via <GlossaryLink term="Phase-Locked Loop (PLL)">PLL</GlossaryLink>, and whose dynamic behaviour is opaque to the 400 kV SCADA, that paradigm has reached the limits of its management capacity.

28 April 2025 is not merely the case study of a blackout: it is the event that marks the **exhaustion of the centralised dispatch model** in systems with high power electronics penetration, and the mandatory starting point of a new architecture of distributed, real-time control for the 21st-century European grid.
