<GlitchTitle>Analysis of Official Reports</GlitchTitle>

The cross-analysis of the four reports on the April 28 blackout reveals an architecture of disputes that goes beyond specific technical disagreements: three radically different institutional views on the same sequence of events, with direct legal, economic, and regulatory implications. This chapter examines the internal coherence of each narrative, their verified points of consensus, and their irreconcilable contradictions.

## The Government and REE's View: Multifactorial Causality and Regulatory Non-Compliance

The official narrative characterizes the blackout not as an operational design error or a direct consequence of dispatch maneuvers, but as a multifactorial event: the convergence of valley demand, massive IBR penetration, and inadequate technical behavior by private agents that, acting simultaneously, exceeded the thresholds of the N−1 Criterion.

<ForensicTable

| Factor | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Insufficient voltage control | Non-compliance with P.O. 7.4 by synchronous generators and RCR |
| Electromechanical oscillations | Conditioning factors of the system state prior to collapse |
| Generation disconnections | Some classified as premature → final vector of overvoltage |

</ForensicTable>


REE's technical defense of the meshing maneuver deserves precise examination. The report highlights an aspect frequently omitted in criticisms: the initial oscillations did not cause overvoltages but voltage drops at transmission network nodes. Faced with this risk, the Control Center executed strictly protocolized actions — reconnection of circuits, coupling of reactors, coordination with RTE to switch the HVDC to PMODE1 — acknowledging that these maneuvers "contributed to the rise in voltages," but classifying them as necessary. REE argues that any subsequent voltage increase would have been absorbable if the agents had fulfilled their regulatory obligations.

The central argument shifts responsibility to the generation fleet: the analysis of the 850 largest generation facilities documents that approximately 22% did not meet the power factor requirement under RD 413/2014, although the report itself notes that part of this non-compliance could be attributed to the capacitive effect of evacuation infrastructures with low generation. Notably, one thermal power plant in the southern zone not only failed to absorb inductive reactive power but injected additional capacitive reactive power. For REE, the collective inhibition of the private fleet deprived the system of the dynamic margin needed to contain the transient.

Regarding inertia, REE explicitly rules it out as the material cause: the system operated with $H = 2.3$ s, above the 2 s threshold<KeyFact id="inercia-28a" mode="callback">H = 2.3 s average / 1.3 s southern zone — Chapter 2</KeyFact> recommended by ENTSO-E, and "the overvoltage wave would have caused the cascade effect in any case," since the overvoltage conditions persisted until 12:33:23 CEST regardless of frequency.

The governmental narrative shows appreciable internal coherence and identifies documented regulatory non-compliance. However, it does not address two arguments from the ICAI expert report: the magnitude of capacitive reactive power injected by the meshing and the operator's unobservability of the 220 kV network due to the Tap-Lag phenomenon — two elements that, according to ICAI, would have made the collapse inevitable regardless of the generators' level of compliance.

## The Generation Sector's View (ICAI / AELEC): Excessive Meshing and Inevitable Collapse

In contrast to the governmental narrative, the expert opinion of IIT-ICAI together with Compass Lexecon and INESC TEC formally diagnoses the event as an overvoltage collapse: a voltage instability phenomenon driven by the exhaustion of reactive power absorption capacity, in which REE's own preventive actions acted as the primary vector of the cascade.

The core of the criticism is quantitative: ICAI simulations estimate that the meshing contracted the margin to collapse at the Carmona node from 2,964 MW to 1,268 MW — a reduction of 57%. <KeyFact id="mallado-ferranti" mode="callback">physics of meshing and Ferranti effect — Chapter 3, §Phase 0</KeyFact>

![Q-V curves at the Carmona 400 kV node according to the ICAI expert analysis](/figuras/fluctuaciones_tension_previas.png)

_Figure E2 — Q-V voltage stability curves at the Carmona 400 kV node (ICAI expert analysis). The diagram quantifies the contraction of the margin to collapse induced by the meshing maneuvers: from 2,964 MW to 1,268 MW, a 57% reduction, which the expert report classifies as the condition that made the collapse mathematically inevitable. Source: IIT-ICAI / Compass Lexecon._

The discrepancy between ICAI's figures (2.4 GVAr) and the independent NREL analysis (1.05 GVAr) does not alter the shared conclusion: in both cases, the capacitive injection exceeded the system's residual absorption capacity, estimated at barely 3.3 GVAr compared to the usual 5.8 GVAr.

Regarding the cascade trips, the generation sector refutes the classification of "inadequate trips" by pointing out the operator's observability limitation. The **Tap-Lag** phenomenon amplified the overvoltage towards the 220 kV networks, invisible to REE's SCADA. <KeyFact id="fase-2-taplag" mode="callback">complete physical mechanism of Tap-Lag — Chapter 3, §Phase 2</KeyFact> The owner of the Granada infrastructure confirmed that its protection "was correctly set and functioned adequately" given the actual voltages experienced at its connection point.<KeyFact id="granada-disparo" mode="callback">root trip Granada — Chapter 3</KeyFact>

This blind spot is what makes the narratives of REE and the generation sector technically incompatible: without observability over the underlying network, the operator could not correct what it did not see, and the generators could not stop tripping on what they did see. The discrepancy is not, therefore, about the facts — it is about the attribution of responsibility given an instrumental limitation that both parties acknowledge.

![Oscillographic record of the root trip in Granada at 12:32:56.993 CEST](/figuras/aluvion_alertas_sobretension_sur.png)

_Figure E3 — Oscillographic record of the root trip in Granada (12:32:56.993 CEST). The waveform shows the voltage on the secondary collector phase A ≈ 145 kV (>1.10 p.u.), a threshold that correctly activated the overvoltage protections but was invisible to REE's SCADA due to the Tap-Lag effect, creating the operational blind spot that made preventive intervention impossible. Source: IIT-ICAI / AELEC._

The second line of argument points to the regulatory framework: P.O. 7.4, in its wording prior to the reform, required IBRs to operate with a fixed power factor, preventing their power electronics from dynamically absorbing or injecting reactive power despite having that technological capability. This restriction excluded 82% of instantaneous generation from active voltage control, concentrating responsibility on the scarce available synchronous thermal fleet.

The resulting quantitative imbalance was, according to the expert report, mathematically insurmountable: in the southern zone — the epicenter of the oscillations and meshing — REE had barely 0.2 GVAr of inductive reactive absorption capacity against an estimated capacitive injection exceeding 0.7 GVAr induced by its own maneuvers.

![Reactive power balance in the southern zone at 12:30 CEST according to IIT-ICAI](/figuras/asimetria_balance_reactiva_sur.png)

_Figure E4 — Reactive power balance in the southern zone at 12:30 CEST. The diagram documents a net deficit of −0.6 GVAr: barely 0.2 GVAr of inductive absorption capacity against a capacitive injection exceeding 0.7 GVAr induced by the meshing maneuvers, a condition that the ICAI expert report classifies as mathematically insurmountable. Source: IIT-ICAI / Compass Lexecon._

The expert report's conclusion identifies three factors directly attributable to the operator and regulator: the meshing that saturated the Q-V margins, the structural unobservability of the 220 kV network due to Tap-Lag, and the regulatory framework (P.O. 7.4) that prevented 82% of the fleet from participating in dynamic voltage control.

## The European View (ENTSO-E): Insufficiency of NC RfG and Separation of the Synchronous System

The ENTSO-E expert panel provides the most structural perspective of the three. Its central thesis rejects that the blackout was due to renewable penetration _per se_: the collapse was not a consequence of an excess of renewable generation or a primary inertial shortage, but of the limitations of the voltage profile control framework. The incident highlights the asymmetry between the accelerated deployment of renewable assets and the partial obsolescence of the grid codes governing their dynamic behavior.

The analysis identifies a fundamental technical tension: most of the installations that disconnected had the necessary power electronics to perform robust voltage control, but the current Spanish regulations prohibited or did not require them to provide this dynamic service, forcing them to operate with static power factors under RD 413/2014. This regulatory restriction deprived the system of the distributed reactive support that could have dampened the capacitive injection from the unloaded lines.

As a direct response, ENTSO-E has consolidated in its Phase II Report the update of NC RfG 2.0, which plans to impose grid-forming as a mandatory requirement for all non-synchronous modules ≥ 1 MW:

Regarding the topology of the incident, ENTSO-E analyzes the loss of synchronism at 12:33:19 CEST: the system went from exporting 469 MW (12:32:57) to importing a maximum of 3,807 MW (12:33:19), the moment of loss of synchronism. The angular pole divergence became unsustainable and the Out-of-Step (OST) protections acted at 12:33:21 CEST, ordering the opening of the cross-Pyrenean circuits (Baixas-Vic, Argia-Arkale, Argia-Hernani).

ENTSO-E's final warning is structural: the coordinated security analyses (CSA) and common grid models (CGM), based on static load flows and the N−1 Criterion, evaluated the system as "Normal" until the moments before the collapse. For ENTSO-E, this limitation indicates that the continental operational security assessment standards, designed for conventional synchronous machines, are structurally insufficient to prevent collapses in systems with high power electronics penetration.

## Interpretive Synthesis: Three Governance Fractures

The comparative scrutiny allows us to transcend the dispute between parties and formulate a second-order diagnosis: the April 28 blackout was neither an unpredictable "black swan" nor the failure of a specific technology, but the convergent manifestation of three governance fractures that coexisted in the Iberian electricity system.

### Operational Fracture: Causality vs. Responsibility

The thesis of meshing as the trigger (ICAI) and the thesis of collective non-compliance as the cause (REE / Government) are not mutually exclusive in technical terms: both factors contributed to the capacitive saturation that narrowed the Q-V margins. What are mutually exclusive are their legal and economic implications. This causal ambiguity is the predictable result of operating a network without sufficient dynamic instrumentation: when the system lacks PMUs at all critical nodes and real-time voltage stability calculation tools, forensic reconstruction inevitably remains subject to interpretation. The tacit consensus among the parties — which no report explicitly states — is that the monitoring architecture proved insufficient both for managing the incident in real time and for resolving it unequivocally a posteriori.

### Regulatory Fracture: 20th Century Regulations Applied to a 21st Century Grid

ENTSO-E's position points to the fact that both the operator and the generators acted within the limits of regulations that were inadequate for the system they were intended to govern. P.O. 7.4 and RD 413/2014 designed a voltage control scheme for a network dominated by slow-response synchronous masses; applying it to a network with 82% IBR penetration is equivalent, in control engineering terms, to trying to stabilize a system with millisecond response times using a regulator designed for minute-scale dynamics. This inadequacy was not unknown: the proposed update of P.O. 7.4 had been in the regulatory approval process for years. The Government's own report acknowledges that its entry into force would have been the most relevant change to have avoided the collapse. The April 28 blackout is, in this sense, the measurable cost of a regulatory delay.

### Systemic Fracture: Static Security Tools in a Dynamic Grid

The unanimous consensus on the insufficiency of the static N−1 Criterion points to a vulnerability that transcends the Iberian case. The load flow models that evaluated the system as "Normal" are mathematically incapable of representing capacitive instability phenomena in low-inertia networks: they do not solve differential equations, do not model the dynamics of inverter control loops, and do not calculate Q-V margins in real time. The speed of the collapse progression — from the first trips to voltage collapse in less than 90 seconds — places the phenomenon outside the time horizon of any human intervention: by the time the operator perceived the severity of the transient, the system was already unrecoverable. The only viable response is structural prevention through real-time dynamic security analysis, the deployment of which in European regional coordination centers constitutes one of the most urgent reforms identified by ENTSO-E.

### The Limit of the Centralized Paradigm

Underlying the three fractures is a deeper tension that the reports address only fragmentarily: the centralized operation paradigm — a single operator with limited visibility at the 400 kV level, managing voltage with discrete switching reactors and dispatching synchronous generation via hourly market signals — was designed for a grid where synchronous mass provided natural damping and generators responded in seconds. In a grid where 82% of generation consists of inverters that respond in milliseconds, self-provide synchronism references via PLLs, and whose dynamic behavior is opaque to the 400 kV SCADA, this paradigm has reached the limits of its management capacity.

April 28 is not merely a blackout case study: it is the event that signals the exhaustion of the centralized dispatch model in systems with high power electronics penetration and the mandatory starting point for a new architecture of distributed, real-time control for the 21st-century European grid.
