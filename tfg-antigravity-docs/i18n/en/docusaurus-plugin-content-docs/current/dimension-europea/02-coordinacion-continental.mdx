<GlitchTitle>Continental Coordination</GlitchTitle>

The abrupt separation of the Iberian Peninsula from the Continental Europe synchronous system was not just an infrastructure incident: it was the first real-world test of the pan-European power grid governance system designed after the great European blackout of 2006. This chapter documents how that institutional architecture responded — its speeds, its successes, and its limits — from the first seconds of the collapse to the final resynchronization.

## Synchronous Area Monitors (SAM): activation of unified command

The ENTSO-E governance architecture designates **Synchronous Area Monitors (SAM)** to assume macroscopic coordination control in events threatening the integrity of Continental Europe. In April 2025, these roles fell to *Swissgrid* (Switzerland) and *Amprion* (Germany). The April 28 contingencies in Spain, Portugal, and France fell directly under Swissgrid's supervisory domain.

The ENTSO-E Awareness System (EAS) — an encrypted, centralized communications platform — operated as the nervous system of crisis management. The chronology of dispatches reveals the speed at which a unified supranational command was structured:

The critical window lies between **12:49 and 12:54 CEST**: in just five minutes, the SAMs applied the ENTSO-E standardized system separation protocol, assigning three distinct roles with exclusive and non-overlapping authority:

**REE — Iberian Frequency Leader.** Exclusive mandate to balance the generation/demand ratio within the isolated block, managing load shedding and gradual restart without external interference.

**Swissgrid — Continental Europe Frequency Leader.** Control of the rest of the synchronous area, orchestrating Frequency Containment Reserves (FCR) across more than 20 countries to compensate for the sudden overfrequency generated by the loss of exports to the peninsula and the shutdown of the Baixas HVDC.

**RTE — Resynchronization Leader.** Absolute authority over closing operations on the trans-Pyrenean interconnections. Neither REE nor any other entity could operate a border switch without prior validation of slip and voltage parameters from RTE's control rooms in Paris and Toulouse.

## The role of Coreso: the mathematical shield of restoration

While national TSOs maneuvered physical switches, the Regional Coordination Center (RCC) Coreso (Brussels) performed immense computational work to ensure that crisis solutions did not trigger new blackouts.

As the afternoon progressed, emergency power shipments from France to Spain escalated, with transitional imports of up to **2,000 MW** being negotiated. These massive injections severely modify natural load flows across French, Swiss, and German networks. Coreso, using **Coordinated Security Analysis (CSA)** tools, processed AC load flows on the **Common Grid Model (CGM)** almost in real time.

Its objective was to verify strict adherence to the N−1 Criterion: certify through simulations that if, while France was exporting those 2,000 MW of support, a French or German internal corridor were suddenly lost, the remaining interconnected network would not suffer thermal overloads or voltage excursions. Simultaneously, the Nordic RCC led the **Short-Term Adequacy Analysis (STA)**, confirming to the SAMs that sufficient spinning capacity was available in central Europe to sustain this effort without jeopardizing the evening demand peak of the donor countries.

This validation by Coreso was the **mathematical shield** that allowed RTE to operate with tactical confidence. Without it, the risk of instability propagating from the peninsula into the core of the continental network would have forced severe limitations on support, slowing recovery.

## The limitations the EAS could not conceal

Multinational coordination functioned with notable effectiveness once the emergency state was activated. However, the episode starkly exposed the structural limitations of the European monitoring system in the pre-fault phase: as documented in the analysis of the Spanish system, the Iberian block transitioned directly from the "Normal" state to the "Blackout" state (OB3 criterion) without passing through the intermediate Alert or Emergency states foreseen in the System Operation Guidelines.

The result was that neighboring operators — RTE, Swissgrid, Coreso — received no anticipatory signals of dynamic vulnerability south of the Pyrenees. The first action of the SAM/EAS architecture was not preventive but reactive: the EAS acted as a post-mortem notifier of the collapse, not as an early warning tool. This limitation — the inability of the European monitoring system to capture fast voltage dynamics in low-inertia networks — is one of the central regulatory lessons of the incident and the starting point for the reforms documented in the following section.

Communication remained constant until the end of the emergency, culminating at **00:11 CEST on April 29**, when RTE notified Swissgrid that REE's Automatic Generation Control (AGC) algorithm was again in a stabilized closed loop — a signal certifying the end of the continental emergency state.
