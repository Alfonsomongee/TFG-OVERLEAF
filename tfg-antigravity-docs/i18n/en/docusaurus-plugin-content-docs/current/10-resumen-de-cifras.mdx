## The largest blackout in the continental European system in over 20 years

April 28, 2025, will be recorded as the date the Iberian peninsula experienced an unprecedented total electrical collapse in modern European history. In just 32 seconds —between 12:32:57 and 12:33:29 CEST— a cascade of generation disconnections engulfed peninsular Spain and continental Portugal in a blackout that interrupted the supply of demand on the order of 25.2 GW to approximately 57 million people.

What follows is a numerical summary of the incident through six interactive visualizations that document the magnitude of the event, its technical causes, the mechanism of its development, and the exceptional operational response that allowed recovery in approximately 18.5 hours.

---

### 1. The immediate impact: Six figures that define a crisis

<Bloque1KPI />

The collapse was so rapid and total that most regional operators had no time to implement corrective maneuvers. The first generation trips —occurring between seconds 0 and 20 of the cascade— eliminated approximately 2,500 MW of generation capacity from a peninsular grid operating with a demand in the range of 29.6 GW.

But these figures, although enormous, hide a more subtle reality: the blackout was not caused by a lack of available energy (the system had sufficient generation), but by a catastrophic failure in voltage control of a grid where the vast majority of generation was operated by power electronics without dynamic stabilization capacity (the 82% detailed in §2.3).

---

### 2. Generation at the time of collapse: An unbalanced system

<Bloque2MixGeneracion />

At 12:30 CEST, moments before the collapse, the Spanish peninsular system operated with a total generation on the order of 29.6 GW. The composition of that generation revealed an extreme concentration in technologies based on electronic converters, particularly in solar photovoltaic energy (see [**Table 15**](./15-galeria-de-tablas.mdx) in the Table Gallery for the exact metric breakdown by technology).

This distribution is the structural context of the incident. Synchronous generation —that is, rotating machines (nuclear, hydro, gas turbines) that produce alternating current by electromagnetic rotation— represented only 18% of the instantaneous mix. The remaining 82% operated through electronic power inverters (mostly Solar PV + Wind) configured at that time for reactive power control in "fixed power factor" mode, without dynamic voltage adjustment capability.

This imbalance is fundamental to understanding why the blackout occurred. In a traditional 20th-century grid, dominated by synchronous generation, a sudden loss of approximately 2,500 MW would have caused a severe but controllable frequency drop through the rotational inertia of the generators (typically H > 3 seconds). In the Iberian peninsula on April 28, the equivalent inertia of the system was only in the range of 2.3 seconds, barely sufficient for regulatory margins, and the response to the disturbance depended entirely on the ability of the inverters to sustain voltage and phase angle. When that capability collapsed, the system collapsed with it.

### 4. The frequency drop: The window of instability

<Bloque4Frecuencia />

System frequency is the vital pulse of an electrical grid. In normal operation, it maintains a stable value of 50 Hz (in continental Europe). Every 0.1 Hz deviation triggers low or high-level protections, because a frequency deviation indicates an imbalance between demand and generation.

During the 27 seconds of the collapse, the frequency behaved like an electrocardiogram of a dying system:

- **Seconds −2 to 0**: Stable at 50.00 Hz. Normal system.
- **Seconds 0–19**: Slow drop to 49.95 Hz. The system absorbs the first trip (Granada, −355 MW) without immediate alarm.
- **Second 19.34 onwards**: Accelerated drop. At 21 seconds: 49.30 Hz. Pumping UFLS protections are activated (49.5 Hz, then 49.3 Hz).
- **Second 22**: 49.00 Hz. Activation of industrial transport load UFLS. The system attempts load shedding (reduce demand) but the rate of change of frequency (ROCOF) is higher than what protections can handle.
- **Second 23.53**: 48.46 Hz. AC trip France. Import is lost. ROCOF remains ≤ 1 Hz/s, but the margin is zero.
- **Second 24**: 47.79 Hz (nadir). Activation of nuclear plants and HVDC blocking.
- **Second 24.5 onwards**: Collapse. Zero voltage. Undefined frequency.

The maximum recorded ROCOF (Rate of Change of Frequency) was approximately 1.0–1.2 Hz/s in 100-millisecond windows during the cascade, exceeding at several moments the dynamic margins that inverters can follow. This is the physical essence of the incident: a grid with 82% electronic generation facing a disturbance that unfolded faster than that generation could respond to.

### 5. Interconnections: The geographical factor that confined the crisis

<Bloque5Interconexiones />

One of the structural factors of the collapse was the low interconnection of the Iberian peninsula with the rest of the continental European system at 12:30 CEST (the meticulous breakdown of physical flows, exports, and saturation degrees of each cross-border link is detailed in [**Table 14**](./15-galeria-de-tablas.mdx) and [**Forensic Charts 1-5**](./16-galeria-forense.mdx)).

The interconnection ratio —the quotient between import capacity and total demand— was approximately 3–5%. This means that, even in the best scenario, peninsular Spain had an external support capacity of only in the range of 900 MW to 1,500 MW for a system with a typical demand of 25–30 GW. That is, approximately 3–5 minutes of coverage in case of loss of internal generation.

During the cascade (12:33:19), when the system attempted to import massively from France to sustain voltage, the inverters requested transient flows of up to 4,609 MVAr (reactive power), which exceeded the safe operation limits of the AC interconnection, whose nominal active power capacity is 2,700 MW (equivalent thermal limit on the order of 3,000 MVA in apparent power). This accelerated the propagation of instability towards France, activating ENTSO-E border isolation protections (AC lines trip at 48.46 Hz, 12:33:21.535).

Unlike Italy (2003) or France (2006), where greater interconnection allowed a quick distribution of the recovery effort, the Iberian peninsula remained confined and isolated, forcing an autonomous restoration from zero.

### 6. Restoration: approximately 18.5 hours from electrical zero

<Bloque6Cronologia />

Barely 2 minutes after electrical zero (12:35 CEST), Red Eléctrica Española requested EDP Portugal to independently start the Castelo do Bode hydroelectric plant (138 MW, Zêzere basin). This action was the first step of what would become one of the most complex restoration operations ever recorded in Europe.

The chronology was as follows:

**12:43–12:44 CEST (Duration: 10–11 minutes from zero)**
Re-energization of the first transmission artery: the Hernani substation in the Basque Country receives supply from the interconnection with France. Initial import: on the order of 31 MW (barely 0.1% of pre-blackout demand).

**12:45 CEST (WEST: 11:45, _Western European Summer Time_ used in Portugal)**
Castelo do Bode (Portugal) successfully connects to the REN 220 kV busbar. Autonomous start in approximately 10 minutes. First Portuguese electrical island created. REN operators begin gradual expansion towards Lisbon and the Tagus.

**13:35 CEST**
Synchronization of the Baixàs–Vic line (France–Catalonia, 400 kV east). Second artery from France. Expansion towards the Mediterranean coast.

**18:36 CEST (WEST: 17:36)**
Aldeadávila–Pocinho synchronization (220 kV): First Spain–Portugal high voltage interconnection. Portugal connects to the continental European frequency. Spanish black-start schemes (BS-Duero 1, BS-Extremadura, BS-Aragón) activated in chain since approximately 16:00 CEST.

**23:00 CEST (Duration: approximately 10.5 hours from zero)**
Recovery: 51% of peninsular demand (12,847 MW active). 70% of transmission substations energized. Hospitals, essential services, and most distribution networks restored. Still around 8% of the population without supply, mainly in isolated rural areas.

**00:22 CEST – April 29**
Portuguese transmission grid 100% restored (total duration for Portugal: approximately 12 hours).

**04:00 CEST – April 29**
Spanish transmission grid 100% restored. Accumulated time from zero: approximately 15.5 hours. Distribution in the last mile restoration process.

**07:00 CEST – April 29 (Total duration: approximately 18.5 hours)**
99.95% of peninsular demand recovered. Barely small isolated rural areas still without coverage. Completely stable system. Normal voltage and frequency. Operational end of the incident.

**Autonomous start (black-start) plants were the bottleneck and the critical factor in the restoration:**

- **Castelo do Bode (Portugal, EDP)**: Hydroelectric, 138 MW. Started in approximately 10 minutes thanks to water stored in the Zêzere basin. Cost of black-start service: in the range of 240,000 €/year (Portuguese Government announced extension to 2030 and incorporation of Alqueva and Baixo Sabor on April 29).
- **Aldeadávila (Spain, Iberdrola)**: Reversible hydroelectric in the Duero, approximately 1,100 MW (pump + turbine). BS-Duero scheme activated around 16:00 CEST. Controlled start capacity without external support guaranteed by stored water and gradual synchronization capability.

The Iberian system, in retrospect, was saved by the luck that two strategically located hydroelectric plants had autonomous start capacity. Without Castelo do Bode and Aldeadávila, restoration would have required sustained import from France, an issue that would have complicated operational coordination with RTE and ENTSO-E for additional critical hours.

## Conclusion: From figures to structure

The six preceding visualizations are not mere numbers. They are the translation of a profound systemic failure: a modern network, decarbonized, efficient in production, but fatally unbalanced in its generation composition, controlled by protection systems designed for a 20th-century paradigm that no longer existed.

The blackout of April 28, 2025, was inevitable given the parameters of that day. But it was avoidable with long-term policies: greater synchronous inertia, better interconnection, mandatory dynamic control in renewables, and —more fundamentally— an energy transition speed that respected the limits of inertia and stability of the grid.

The lesson, recorded in 27 seconds of cascade and approximately 18.5 hours of restoration, has reshaped the European electrical architecture. April 28, 2025, was the day the continental electrical engineering had to admit that the 20th century had ended and the future required new laws, not just new machines.
