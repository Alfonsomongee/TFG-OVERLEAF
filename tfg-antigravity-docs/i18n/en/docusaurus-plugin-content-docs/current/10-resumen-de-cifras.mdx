## The largest blackout of the continental European system in over 20 years

April 28, 2025, will be etched in history as the date the Iberian Peninsula experienced a total electrical collapse unprecedented in modern European history. In just 32 seconds — between 12:32:57 and 12:33:29 CEST — a cascade of generation disconnections engulfed mainland Spain and continental Portugal in a blackout that interrupted supply to approximately 25.2 GW of demand for roughly 57 million people.

What follows is a numerical summary of the incident through six interactive visualizations documenting the event's magnitude, its technical causes, the mechanism of its development, and the exceptional operational response that enabled recovery in approximately 18.5 hours.

---

### 1. The immediate impact: Six figures defining a crisis

<Bloque1KPI />

The collapse was so rapid and total that most regional operators had no time to implement corrective maneuvers. The initial generation trips — occurring between seconds 0 and 20 of the cascade — eliminated ≈2,500 MW of generation capacity from a peninsular network dispatching ≈29.6 GW of generation against approximately 25.2 GW of net demand.

But these figures, though enormous, conceal a more subtle reality: the blackout was not caused by a lack of available energy (the system had sufficient generation), but by a catastrophic failure in voltage control of a network where the vast majority of generation was operated by power electronics without dynamic stabilization capability (the 82% detailed in §2.3).

---

### 2. Generation at the moment of collapse: An unbalanced system

<Bloque2MixGeneracion />

At 12:30 CEST, moments before the collapse, the Spanish peninsular system was operating with a total generation of approximately 29.6 GW. The composition of that generation revealed an extreme concentration in technologies based on electronic converters, particularly solar photovoltaic (see [Annex I · Demand, generation and balance](./anexo-demanda-generacion-balance.mdx) for the metric breakdown by technology).

This distribution is the structural context of the incident. Synchronous generation — i.e., rotating machines (nuclear, hydro, gas turbines) that produce alternating current through electromagnetic rotation — represented only 18% of the instantaneous mix. The remaining 82% operated through electronic power inverters (mostly Solar PV + Wind) configured at that time for reactive power control in "fixed power factor" mode, without dynamic voltage adjustment capability.

This imbalance is fundamental to understanding why the blackout occurred. In a traditional 20th-century network, dominated by synchronous generation, a sudden loss of ≈2,500 MW would have caused a severe but controllable frequency drop through the rotational inertia of generators (typically H > 3 seconds). On the Iberian Peninsula on April 28, the system's equivalent inertia was only about 2.3 seconds, barely sufficient for regulatory margins, and the response to the disturbance depended entirely on the inverters' ability to sustain voltage and phase angle. When that capability collapsed, the system collapsed with it.

The incident unfolded in a strictly chronological sequence that crossed the point of no return at 24 seconds. ### 4. The frequency drop: The instability window

<Bloque4Frecuencia />

System frequency is the vital pulse of an electrical grid. In normal operation, it maintains a stable value of 50 Hz (in continental Europe). Every 0.1 Hz deviation triggers under- or over-frequency protections, because a frequency deviation indicates an imbalance between demand and generation.

During the 27 seconds of the collapse, the frequency behaved like an electrocardiogram of a dying system:

- Seconds −2 to 0: Stable at 50.00 Hz. Normal system.
- Seconds 0–19: Slow drop to 49.95 Hz. The system absorbs the first trip (Granada, −355 MW) without immediate alarm.
- Second 19.34 onwards: Accelerated drop. At 21 seconds: 49.30 Hz. Pumping UFLS protections activate (49.5 Hz, then 49.3 Hz).
- Second 22: 49.00 Hz. Activation of industrial transport load UFLS. The system attempts to shed load (reduce demand) but the rate of change of frequency (ROCOF) is faster than what protections can handle.
- Second 23.53: 48.46 Hz. France AC trip. Import is lost. ROCOF remains ≤ 1 Hz/s, but the margin is zero.
- Second 24: 47.79 Hz (nadir). Activation of nuclear plants and HVDC blocking.
- Second 24.5 onwards: Collapse. Zero voltage. Undefined frequency.

The maximum recorded ROCOF was approximately 1.0–1.2 Hz/s in 100-millisecond windows during the cascade, exceeding at several points the dynamic margins that inverters can follow. This is the physical essence of the incident: a network with 82% electronic generation facing a disturbance that unfolded faster than that generation could respond.

### 5. Interconnections: The geographic factor that confined the crisis

<Bloque5Interconexiones />

One of the structural factors of the collapse was the low interconnection of the Iberian Peninsula with the rest of the continental European system at 12:30 CEST (a detailed breakdown of physical flows, exports, and saturation levels of each cross-border link is documented in [Annex IV · Interconnections and flows](./anexo-interconexiones-flujos.mdx)).

The interconnection ratio — the quotient between import capacity and total demand — was approximately 3–5%. This means that, even in the best scenario, mainland Spain had external support capacity of only ≈900 MW to ≈1,500 MW for a system with 25–30 GW of typical demand. That is, approximately 3–5 minutes of coverage in the event of a loss of internal generation.

During the cascade (12:33:19), when the system attempted to import massively from France to sustain voltage, the inverters requested transient flows of up to 4,609 MVAr (reactive power), which exceeded the safe operating limits of the AC interconnection, whose nominal active power capacity is 2,700 MW (equivalent thermal limit ≈3,000 MVA in apparent power). This accelerated the propagation of instability towards France, activating ENTSO-E border isolation protections (AC line trip at 48.46 Hz, 12:33:21.535).

Unlike Italy (2003) or France (2006), where greater interconnection allowed rapid distribution of recovery effort, the Iberian Peninsula remained confined and isolated, forcing an autonomous restoration from zero.

### 6. Restoration: Approximately 18.5 hours from electrical zero

<Bloque6Cronologia />

Just 2 minutes after electrical zero (12:35 CEST), Red Eléctrica Española requested EDP Portugal to autonomously start the Castelo do Bode hydroelectric plant (138 MW, Zêzere basin). This action was the first step in what would become one of the most complex restoration operations ever recorded in Europe.

The timeline was as follows:

12:43–12:44 CEST (Duration: 10–11 minutes from zero)
Re-energization of the first transport artery: the Hernani substation in the Basque Country receives supply from the interconnection with France. Initial import: ≈31 MW (barely 0.1% of pre-blackout demand).

12:45 CEST (WEST: 11:45, Western European Summer Time used in Portugal)
Castelo do Bode (Portugal) successfully connects to REN's 220 kV busbar. Autonomous start in approximately 10 minutes. First Portuguese electrical island created. REN operators begin gradual expansion towards Lisbon and the Tagus.

13:35 CEST
Synchronization of the Baixàs–Vic line (France–Catalonia, 400 kV east). Second artery from France. Expansion towards the Mediterranean coast.

18:36 CEST (WEST: 17:36)
Synchronization of Aldeadávila–Pocinho (220 kV): First Spain–Portugal high-voltage interconnection. Portugal connects to the continental European frequency. Spanish black-start schemes (BS-Duero 1, BS-Extremadura, BS-Aragón) activated in sequence from approximately 16:00 CEST.

23:00 CEST (Duration: approximately 10.5 hours from zero)
Recovery: 51% of peninsular demand (12,847 MW active). 70% of transport substations energized. Hospitals, essential services, and most distribution networks restored. Still ≈8% of population without supply, mainly in isolated rural areas.

00:22 CEST – April 29
Portuguese transport network 100% restored (total duration for Portugal: approximately 12 hours).

04:00 CEST – April 29
Spanish transport network 100% restored. Cumulative time from zero: approximately 15.5 hours. Distribution in last-mile restoration process.

07:00 CEST – April 29 (Total duration: approximately 18.5 hours)
99.95% of peninsular demand recovered. Only small isolated rural areas still without coverage. System completely stable. Normal voltage and frequency. Operational end of the incident.

Black-start plants were the bottleneck and critical factor in the restoration:

- Castelo do Bode (Portugal, EDP): Hydroelectric, 138 MW. Started in approximately 10 minutes thanks to reservoir water in the Zêzere basin. Cost of black-start service: ≈€240,000/year (Portuguese government announced extension to 2030 and incorporation of Alqueva and Baixo Sabor on April 29).
- Aldeadávila (Spain, Iberdrola): Reversible hydroelectric on the Duero, ≈1,100 MW (pump + turbine). BS-Duero scheme activated approximately at 16:00 CEST. Controlled start capability without external support guaranteed by reservoir water and gradual synchronization capacity.

In retrospect, the Iberian system was saved by the luck that two strategically located hydroelectric plants had autonomous start capability. Without Castelo do Bode and Aldeadávila, restoration would have required sustained import from France, an issue that would have complicated operational coordination with RTE and ENTSO-E during additional critical hours.

## Conclusion: From figures to structure

The six visualizations above are not mere numbers. They are the translation of a deep systemic failure: a modern, decarbonized network, efficient in production, but fatally unbalanced in its generation composition, controlled by protection systems designed for a 20th-century paradigm that no longer existed.

The blackout of April 28, 2025, was highly probable given the operating parameters of that day. But it was avoidable with long-term policies: greater synchronous inertia, better interconnection, mandatory dynamic control in renewables, and — more fundamentally — an energy transition speed that respected the inertia and stability limits of the grid.

The lesson, etched in 27 seconds of cascade and approximately 18.5 hours of restoration, has reshaped the European electrical architecture. April 28, 2025, was the day continental electrical engineering had to admit that the 20th century had ended and the future required new laws, not just new machines.
