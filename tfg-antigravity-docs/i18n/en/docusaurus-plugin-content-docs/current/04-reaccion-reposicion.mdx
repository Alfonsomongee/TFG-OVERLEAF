Following the zero-voltage event at 12:33:30 CEST<KeyFact id="granada-disparo" mode="callback">root cause sequence — Chapter 3</KeyFact>, the Spanish peninsular power system transitioned from an uncontrollable dynamic crisis to a phase of emergency management and structural restoration. The loss of over 15 GW and the total disconnection of the continental synchronous system forced REE to abandon normal steady-state operating logic and activate the emergency protocols of Operating Procedure P.O. 1.6. Full supply restoration required approximately 18.5 hours of uninterrupted maneuvers (until 07:05 on 29-Apr, when 99.95% of supply was certified as restored).

## Emergency Management: From P.O. 1.1 to P.O. 1.6

P.O. 1.1 establishes the criteria for steady-state operation, based on compliance with the N−1 Criterion and maintaining voltages between 380 kV and 435 kV on the transmission network. The cascade on 28-Apr revealed the limitations of static validation of these criteria against ultra-fast dynamic transients in low-inertia networks: safety limits were exceeded within an eleven-second window, and automatic defense schemes — including UFLS — proved counterproductive. REE assumed manual control under Blackout status (OB3 criterion).

The institutional reaction was immediate and simultaneous on several fronts:

- 12:34 CEST — REE confirms with REN that the entire Portuguese network is also without voltage.
- 12:36 CEST — Formal notification to other European operators via the EAS, changing the operational status of the Iberian block to 'Restoration'.
- 12:44 CEST — Suspension of all intraday and balancing service markets by REE and OMIE, granting full authority to dispatch solely based on technical feasibility criteria.

The recovery strategy dictated the controlled fragmentation of the Iberian Peninsula into seven independent operational areas — Southern Zone, Tagus-Center, Levante, and others — during the progressive re-energization.

## Black Start Strategy: Hydroelectric Plants and Combined Cycles

With virtually the entire generation fleet composed of IBRs in grid-following mode — technologically incapable of generating a voltage waveform without a stable external grid — the survival and restart of the system relied exclusively on synchronous machines with autonomous start-up capability.

Hydroelectric plants — both run-of-river and pumped-storage — were the first to be dispatched to energize isolated sections and create the initial electrical islands. However, operating these micro-systems revealed the difficulty of operating with minimal inertia: energizing unloaded lines caused significant capacitive transients that prevented several start-up attempts from being completed.

![Temporal deployment and effectiveness of hydroelectric Black Start](/figuras/black_start_hidroelectrico.png)
_Figure D2 — Temporal deployment of hydroelectric Black Start. The figure distinguishes successful and failed energization attempts, showing that the initial restoration depended on synchronous machines capable of forming voltage in electrical islands with low short-circuit power. Source: ENTSO-E / REE._

Failures were significant: the Cantabria and Levante islands could not be sustained and had to be restarted; the plant assigned to Madrid failed to stabilize its parameters after several consecutive attempts; and the autonomous start-up in Andalusia was unsuccessful, forcing prioritization of external support from Morocco as a voltage reference for the southern zone.

Energization transients of unloaded lines generated magnetizing inrush currents of up to 10 times the rated current in 400 kV autotransformers, causing voltage drops of 10–20% in the fragile newly formed islands. Even more problematic was the phenomenon of sympathetic inrush: energizing a new transformer saturated the magnetic flux of already operating parallel transformers, generating severe harmonic distortions with decay times exceeding 10 seconds, causing several islands to collapse before they could stabilize. In at least three documented instances, transformer differential relays interpreted these transients as internal short circuits and ordered tripping, forcing the island process to restart from scratch.

Once the hydroelectric islands managed to minimally stabilize their parameters, the strategy shifted towards their electromechanical consolidation using Natural Gas Combined Cycles (CCGT) and the auxiliary services of nuclear plants. These machines were not connected to meet commercial demand, but to form the 'electromagnetic scaffolding' of the system: they provided the short-circuit power ($S_{sc}$), inertia ($H$), and dynamic reactive power management capability indispensable before authorizing the massive reconnection of demand.

The Portuguese system executed an analogous scheme supported by the HPP 1-Centro hydroelectric plant and the CCGT 1-Norte combined cycle. At 20:22 CEST, the synchronization of the Portuguese islands with the Spanish zones already coupled to the continental European frequency — verified via synchro-check relays — guaranteed the viability of the total restoration of the Iberian Peninsula.

## International Coordination: RCC, Swissgrid, and Continental Resynchronization

The tools of the Regional Coordination Centers (RCC) — particularly Coreso, responsible for Southwestern Europe — assess security using static steady-state load flows, without the capability to anticipate voltage instability phenomena or ultra-fast dynamics associated with low inertia. Consequently, all their pre-collapse analyses returned an 'OK' status, and the Iberian block remained labeled as 'Normal' in the EAS until the very moment of the blackout.

Once the zero-voltage event occurred, the European command hierarchy was activated through the Synchronous Area Monitors (SAM), operated by Swissgrid and Amprion. Between 12:49 and 12:54 CEST, the unified command structure was established:

- REE → frequency leader for the disconnected Iberian island
- Swissgrid → stabilization of the rest of the continent
- RTE (France) → general resynchronization leader

Under this scheme, RTE activated offers in its internal balancing mechanism for up to 4,500 MW to sustain exports to Spain, enabling the energization of the 400 kV corridors in the north and east of the peninsula.

## Mix Evolution and Final Stabilization

Although solar and wind farms were physically intact with available primary resource, their reconnection was restricted during the initial critical phases. Grid-following inverters need to first read a robust external grid to inject current via their PLL loops: without this reference, they are incapable of operating. The entire responsibility for re-energization fell on the sequential coupling of CCGTs, remaining coal plants, and nuclear power plants.

![Evolution of the technological mix during peninsular re-energization](/figuras/evolucion_mix_reenergizacion.png)
_Figure D5 — Evolution of the technological mix during re-energization. The sequence shows that recovery did not initially depend on available IBRs, but on the progressive connection of synchronous generation — hydro, combined cycles, remaining coal, and nuclear — capable of providing voltage reference, inertia, and short-circuit power. Source: ENTSO-E / REE._

Load was reconnected in carefully calculated discrete blocks:

- 13:07 CEST — First 31 MW supplied from the Irún substation.
- 23:32 CEST — With 21 thermal units synchronized, covered demand reached 13,039 MW (≈55% of expected load).
- 00:06 CEST (29-Apr) — REE reactivated the master controller for automatic Frequency Restoration Reserve (aFRR), returning the system's automated secondary regulation capability.
- 01:38 CEST (29-Apr) — First instructions to reintegrate wind farms and cogeneration plants.
- 07:05 CEST (29-Apr) — Lifting of restrictions for the entire RCR regime. REE certifies the restoration of 99.95% of national electricity supply.

### The Grid Healed, the Economy on Pause

At 07:05 on April 29, REE lifted the last operational restrictions. 99.95% of peninsular supply points had voltage. The following graph documents this journey with five-minute resolution: the normal demand profile of the morning of the 28th, the collapse at 12:33 CEST, the flat line of the blackout, and the stepped restoration ramp until normalization in the early hours of the 29th.

That the grid was healed did not mean the economy was. Services and households recovered their normal consumption within hours. Electro-intensive industry — electric arc furnaces, cement plants, refineries — needed a full week. Their continuous processes do not allow immediate restart: quality tests, equipment recalibration, and safety protocols are required before returning to nominal operation. The restoration sequence constitutes an empirical confirmation of the central technical argument of this work: synchronous generation — absent in sufficient quantities during pre-collapse operation — proved to be the only electromechanical support capable of sustaining recovery. Its role as the 'scaffolding' of re-energization is not an operational artifact, but a direct consequence of the fundamental physical properties that grid-following IBRs cannot replicate.
