# Anatomy of the Incident: Chronology of the Collapse (Phases 0 to 3)

## Phase 0: Voltage Instability and Meshing Effect

Forensic examination of the chronology demonstrates that voltage collapse was not an instantaneous event but rather the culmination of progressive degradation of <GlossaryLink term="Estabilidad de tensión">static and dynamic network stability</GlossaryLink>. During Phase 0, analyzers recorded elevated volatility in voltage ($V$) profiles across extremely weak topology: with low demand, 35.8% of the 400 kV network in the southern zone and 34.3% in the central zone remained disconnected, configuring a scenario of high *transfer impedance*.

Over this weakened network, system dynamics manifested as <GlossaryLink term="Oscilaciones electromecánicas">electromechanical oscillations</GlossaryLink> with two distinct modes. At 12:03:00 CEST a first forced oscillation of 0.6 Hz of local character was recorded, which took 4 minutes and 42 seconds to damp. At 12:19:00 CEST a second inter-area mode of 0.2 Hz (East-Central-West) emerged, causing the Iberian peninsula to oscillate in antiphase relative to the rest of the continent, stressing the Pyrenean interconnection. During both transients, the <GlossaryLink term="Damping ratio">*damping ratio*</GlossaryLink> collapsed to values near 1%, violating the minimum 5% threshold required by Procedure 13.1.

![Voltages recorded at Núñez de Balboa 400 kV (April 22, 24, and 28)](/figuras/nunez_balboa_precursores.png)

The previous figure shows voltage recordings at Núñez de Balboa during April 22, 24, and 28. The succession of overvoltage peaks observed is interpreted as evidence of progressive tightening of <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power</GlossaryLink> control margins in the transmission network.

To contain these oscillations, operators executed a meshing maneuver: between 12:03 and 12:30 CEST, REE reconnected 11 400 kV lines that remained open due to low demand. The maneuver achieved its immediate objective—reducing impedance and arresting the "whip" of oscillations—but introduced, according to IIT-ICAI expert analysis, a relevant secondary effect: through the <GlossaryLink term="Efecto Ferranti">*Ferranti Effect*</GlossaryLink>, the reconnected unloaded lines abruptly injected a significant amount of <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">capacitive reactive power</GlossaryLink> into a network that already lacked absorption margin.

Precise quantification of this injection constitutes one of the central axes of technical dispute among reports: the IIT-ICAI expert analysis figures the volume at up to 2.4 GVAr, while the independent analysis by the U.S. National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) adopts a more conservative estimate of 1,050 MVAr. Both figures agree, however, in noting that the injection exceeded the system's residual absorption capacity, estimated at that moment at merely 3.3 GVAr versus the typical 5.8 GVAr.

![WAMS recording of first 0.6 Hz oscillation at 12:03 CEST](/figuras/wams_oscilaciones_carmona.png)

The previous figure shows the <GlossaryLink term="WAMS (Wide Area Monitoring Systems)">WAMS</GlossaryLink> recording of this oscillation. <GlossaryLink term="WAMS (Wide Area Monitoring Systems)">WAMS (*Wide Area Monitoring Systems*)</GlossaryLink> are based on networks of <GlossaryLink term="PMU (Phasor Measurement Unit)">phasor measurement units (PMU)</GlossaryLink> synchronized by GPS, enabling simultaneous observation of network dynamics at continental geographic scales with millisecond resolution.

This massive injection saturated the network's capacity to absorb <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power ($Q$)</GlossaryLink>, reducing safety margins of <GlossaryLink term="Colapso Q-V">voltage stability curves (Q-V)</GlossaryLink>. IIT-ICAI expert simulations estimate that after meshing, the distance to the collapse point at Carmona 400 kV node contracted from a 2,964 MW margin to 1,268 MW, a reduction approaching 57% that brought the system toward a state of capacitive saturation.

## Phase 1: Oscillation Phenomena and Root Trigger (12:32:57 CEST)

The transition toward systemic collapse gestated during the hour prior to the blackout through progressive dynamic instability. Beginning at 12:00:00 CEST, the Iberian system experienced <GlossaryLink term="Oscilaciones electromecánicas">electromechanical oscillations</GlossaryLink> that demonstrated the operational cost of operating with greatly reduced inertia. The first disruptive event was recorded at 12:03:00 CEST: a 0.6 Hz oscillation with 70 mHz amplitude sustained for 4 minutes and 42 seconds before damping.

The nature of this transient is controversial among reports. Red Eléctrica (REE) classifies it as a forced oscillation originating in the control loop of a photovoltaic plant in Badajoz province, while independent expert analysis by IIT-ICAI argues it was a natural inter-area mode exacerbated by a <GlossaryLink term="Damping ratio">systemic *damping ratio*</GlossaryLink> of merely 1%—far below the minimum 5% threshold required by Procedure 13.1—whose origin they link to the absence of *Power System Stabilizers* (PSS) in the combined cycle plants of Andalusia, which remained offline.

![Overall system frequency view during Phase 1](/figuras/timeline_frecuencia_nrel.png)

The previous figure reproduces the external telemetry analysis prepared by NREL, showing the two <GlossaryLink term="Oscilaciones electromecánicas">electromechanical oscillations</GlossaryLink> and progressive loss of systemic damping prior to collapse.

Instability intensified at 12:19:00 CEST with emergence of a second electromechanical mode, this time at 0.2 Hz with maximum amplitude of 200 mHz. European phasor telemetry (<GlossaryLink term="PMU (Phasor Measurement Unit)">PMU, *Phasor Measurement Units*</GlossaryLink>) confirmed this event corresponded to the East-Central-West natural mode of the Continental synchronous area: the reduced Iberian rotating mass began oscillating in antiphase relative to Europe's inertia center, stressing Pyrenean interconnection links.

![Detailed recording of second electromechanical oscillation at 12:19 CEST](/figuras/oscilacion_hernani_icai.png)

Phase divergences observed in the previous figure confirm, according to IIT-ICAI analysis, that the Iberian bloc oscillated in antiphase relative to the European continental synchronous system during minutes preceding the collapse.

To damp these oscillations, the system operator executed two emergency maneuvers: meshing of the 400 kV network and fixing the <GlossaryLink term="HVDC">HVDC</GlossaryLink> link with France in constant power mode (1,000 MW export). Both decisions succeeded in stabilizing frequency, but, according to IIT-ICAI analysis, at significant dynamic cost to voltage stability: the maneuvers saturated the <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">capacitive reactive power</GlossaryLink> network, elevated voltage profiles, and, in expert opinion, substantially reduced safety margins of the <GlossaryLink term="Colapso Q-V">Q-V curves</GlossaryLink>, leaving the system near overvoltage collapse.

The point of no return was reached at 12:32:57 CEST with the "root trigger." A 400/220 kV transformer in a collecting substation in Granada province disconnected due to actuation of its overvoltage protections, detecting approximately 244 kV on the 220 kV secondary, exceeding the equipment's dielectric safety threshold.

![Oscillographic recording of root trigger at 12:32:57](/figuras/disparo_raiz_oscilografia.png)

As visible in the previous figure, the disconnection instantly eliminated a 165 MVAr inductive reactive sink. The magnitude of impact, according to expert analysis, did not reside in the 355 MW of <GlossaryLink term="Potencia activa">active power ($P$)</GlossaryLink> lost with disconnection—a manageable figure for the system—but rather in the instantaneous and irrecoverable loss of 165 MVAr of <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power ($Q$)</GlossaryLink> absorption capacity. In a system saturated with capacitive reactive power and operating with reduced Q-V margins, the disappearance of this sink caused an ascending voltage step at surrounding nodes of the southern transmission network. This scalar rebound was, according to IIT-ICAI analysis, the physical detonator of residual network instability, initiating the chain reaction analyzed in subsequent sections.

## Phase 2: The Tap-Lag Phenomenon and Voltage Decoupling

Phase 2 of the collapse, framed in the 21 seconds following the "root trigger," is characterized by materialization of a cascading failure driven by a mechatronic phenomenon known in literature as the <GlossaryLink term="Tap-Lag">*Tap-Lag*</GlossaryLink> (tap-changer lag) or "observability decoupling." Following the Granada transformer disconnection, abrupt loss of <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power</GlossaryLink> absorption triggered increasing voltage trend in the transmission network, revealing divergence between 400 kV infrastructure behavior and underlying collector networks.

To understand this decoupling mechanics, it is necessary to return to Phase 1. During the morning, the network had experienced various voltage fluctuations and dips originating from frequency oscillations. To counteract these dips and maintain wave quality in renewable generation zones, the 400/220 kV and 400/132 kV interconnection transformers had adjusted their <GlossaryLink term="OLTC (On-Load Tap Changer)">*On-Load Tap Changers* (OLTC)</GlossaryLink>, raising taps to elevate secondary-side voltage.

When sudden overvoltage burst into the 400 kV network, induced by the Granada trigger, these transformers became trapped in their own mechanical inertia. <GlossaryLink term="OLTC (On-Load Tap Changer)">OLTC</GlossaryLink> motor and gear mechanisms are intentionally slowed through time delays to prevent mechanical oscillation loops (*hunting*) against ephemeral disturbances. Consequently, against an ultrafast electrical transient, the tap changers proved excessively slow and could not alter their transformation ratio in time to lower voltage.

![Oscillographic evidence of voltage decoupling](/figuras/tap_lag_decoupling.png)

The previous figure illustrates the phenomenon's decoupling: while 400 kV side voltage increase remained moderate, tap-changer inertia amplified the transient on the collector side.

This mechanical incapacity created a "mirage" in Red Eléctrica's (REE) control room. On <GlossaryLink term="SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition)">SCADA (*Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition*)</GlossaryLink> system screens, the system operator monitored the 400 kV network primary, observing high tensions but theoretically remaining below the normative exceptional limit of 435 kV established in Procedure 1.1. However, due to the <GlossaryLink term="OLTC (On-Load Tap Changer)">OLTC's</GlossaryLink> phase-offset transformation ratio, voltage experienced a multiplier effect toward collector networks.

Downstream, in 220 kV and 132 kV networks, voltage rose above thermal and dielectric limits, reaching thresholds exceeding 1.2 p.u. invisible to national dispatch due to underlying network inobservability.

Confronting this amplification, solar and wind plant inverters faced unsustainable voltages for their power electronics. Facing risk of transistor damage, installations activated internal *Overvoltage Protection* systems (ANSI function 59) to safeguard asset integrity.

![Overvoltage propagation in transmission network (400 kV) during Phase 2](/figuras/heatmap_propagation.png)

This behavior underlies one of the incident's greatest forensic discrepancies: while REE classified these losses as "inappropriate trips" arguing their 400 kV network contained no disconnect thresholds, IIT-ICAI expert analysis contends plants acted correctly and appropriately to standards against real overvoltage experienced at their connection points, downstream of the <GlossaryLink term="OLTC (On-Load Tap Changer)">OLTC</GlossaryLink>-equipped transformer. The <GlossaryLink term="Tap-Lag">*Tap-Lag*</GlossaryLink> phenomenon thus configured, per expert opinion, conditions for a positive feedback loop of cascading disconnections.

## Phase 3: The Path Toward "Voltage Collapse"

Phase 3, spanning the temporal window between 12:33:18 and 12:33:29 CEST, stages the total collapse of the peninsular electrical system in barely eleven seconds. Following onset of <GlossaryLink term="Tap-Lag">*Tap-Lag*</GlossaryLink> trips, the network entered a *feedback loop* of <GlossaryLink term="Colapso Q-V">capacitive instability</GlossaryLink> that no human intervention nor conventional protection automation could arrest.

The physics of this collapse operated through a vicious circle dictated by load flow equations. As massive simultaneous disconnection of inverter-connected plants occurred for self-protection, the network instantaneously lost capacity to absorb <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power ($Q$)</GlossaryLink>. Simultaneously, <GlossaryLink term="Potencia activa">active power ($P$)</GlossaryLink> drop reduced flow in 400 kV lines, increasing their capacitive injection through the <GlossaryLink term="Efecto Ferranti">*Ferranti effect*</GlossaryLink> and pushing voltage above 443 kV at most stressed nodes. Each disconnection raised voltage, forcing new disconnections: the system had entered a state with no possible physical exit.

![Spatial propagation of cascading generation loss during Phase 3](/figuras/cascada_desconexiones.png)

The previous figure summarizes the cascade's geographic propagation. Most severe impacts concentrated initially in Badajoz (more than 725 MW lost at 12:33:16 CEST) and, one second later, at Segovia, Seville, and Huelva substations (930 MW additional at 12:33:17 CEST). In the five-second window between 12:33:19 and 12:33:24 CEST, the overvoltage cascade disconnected more than 15 GW of generation—nearly 60% of national instantaneous demand—carrying the system beyond any possibility of recovery.

As the cascade destroyed remaining generation, frequency ($f$) began to collapse at extreme <GlossaryLink term="RoCoF (Rate of Change of Frequency)">*RoCoF*</GlossaryLink> rate, activating <GlossaryLink term="UFLS (Underfrequency Load Shedding)">*Under-Frequency Load Shedding* (UFLS)</GlossaryLink> schemes. Crossing the 49.5 Hz threshold at 12:33:20 CEST, more than 2,000 MW of hydroelectric pumping were automatically disconnected, followed by multiple industrial demand steps as frequency continued declining to 48.0 Hz.

![Coupled evolution of voltage and frequency during collapse](/figuras/tension_frecuencia_colapso.png)

The previous figure shows both variables' coupled evolution during the phase. It is apparent that voltage increase above 1.10 p.u. precedes the accelerated frequency drop in time, supporting interpretation of the incident as primarily <GlossaryLink term="Colapso Q-V">capacitive</GlossaryLink> rather than inertial collapse.

The <GlossaryLink term="UFLS (Underfrequency Load Shedding)">UFLS</GlossaryLink> action revealed an electromechanical phenomenon already anticipated: the so-called <GlossaryLink term="UFLS (Underfrequency Load Shedding)">*UFLS Paradox*</GlossaryLink>. By cutting active consumption ($P$) to stabilize frequency, the load-shedding scheme simultaneously eliminated <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power ($Q$)</GlossaryLink> consumption from that same demand. In a network saturated with capacitive reactive power, suppressing the last inductive reactive sinks—disconnected motors and industrial loads—produced a new surge of overvoltages, reinforcing the collapse the <GlossaryLink term="UFLS (Underfrequency Load Shedding)">UFLS</GlossaryLink> itself sought to arrest.

In final instants before isolation, massive internal generation deficit caused violent reversal of Pyrenean border flows: AC lines attempted to import from France up to 4,609 MW emergency. However, simultaneously, the <GlossaryLink term="HVDC">HVDC INELFE-1</GlossaryLink> link—fixed in constant power mode (PMODE1) since 12:08 CEST—continued extracting 1,000 MW from the Iberian system, reducing net support capacity from France by 22%. The resulting transit became angularly and thermally unsustainable for the cross-border corridor.

![Loss of synchronism and isolation of the Iberian peninsula](/figuras/interconexion_francia_colapso.png)

The previous figure captures flow reversal at the Pyrenean border during Phase 3, with emergency import of up to 4,609 MW via AC lines, simultaneous extraction by <GlossaryLink term="HVDC">HVDC</GlossaryLink> in constant power mode, and AC line opening due to loss of synchronism at 12:33:21 CEST.

At 12:33:21 CEST, loss-of-synchronism protections opened AC links to prevent contagion to the Continental European system, isolating the Iberian peninsula. Converted into an electrical island lacking voltage self-sustaining capacity, scarce remaining synchronous machines disconnected in cascade. Definitive "Voltage Collapse" was confirmed at 12:33:29.741 CEST with the last generator unit tripping, closing the most severe systemic incident documented in the history of the European continental electrical system.
