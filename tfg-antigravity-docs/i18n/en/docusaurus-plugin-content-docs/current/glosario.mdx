# Technical Glossary

## Chapter 2 — ENTSO-E and Institutions

### ENTSO-E

The European Network of Transmission System Operators for Electricity (ENTSO-E) is the association grouping 40 technical network operators (TSOs) from 36 European countries. Its main mandate, derived from successive EU legislative packages, is to guarantee the security and reliability of the interconnected European power system, facilitate the integration of renewable energy, and establish common Network Codes that are binding on all Member States.

## Chapter 3 — The Spanish Power System

### Transfer impedance

In power systems, the transfer impedance between two nodes represents the electrical opposition to power flow between them. High transfer impedance implies a weakly coupled network, in which small variations in injected power can produce large variations in voltage and phase angle, undermining system stiffness.

### Forced and natural oscillations

An oscillation is _forced_ when induced by a periodic external disturbance — typically a fault or anomalous behaviour in the control loop of a specific piece of equipment — as opposed to _natural oscillations_ or system eigenmodes, whose frequency is determined by the system's own inertia and the electromechanical constants of the connected synchronous machines.

### Damping ratio

The _damping ratio_ (or relative damping) is a dimensionless indicator quantifying how quickly an oscillation attenuates after a disturbance. Values close to 5 % are considered a reasonable operational safety margin in the European synchronous system; values near 0 % indicate sustained oscillations, and negative values imply amplitude growth and, therefore, instability risk.

### Ferranti effect

The _Ferranti effect_ describes the phenomenon whereby, on a high-voltage transmission line operated with little or no load, the voltage at the receiving end exceeds that at the sending end. The cause is the distributed capacitive admittance of the line: with reduced active power flow, the capacitive load is not offset by the inductive consumption of loads, resulting in an overvoltage proportional to the line length. This is especially relevant when energising 400 kV lines at no-load.

### Q-V voltage stability curves

_Q-V curves_ represent, for a given network node, the relationship between the injected or absorbed reactive power and the resulting voltage. The distance between the operating point and the curve minimum (_nose point_) defines the voltage stability margin: the smaller the margin, the greater the risk of voltage collapse under additional disturbances.

### Power System Stabilizers (PSS)

PSS (_Power System Stabilizers_) are additional control loops installed in the excitation systems of large synchronous generators that add electrical damping to the system's electromechanical oscillations. When thermal units with PSS are decoupled — for example, during a spring midday with low demand and high solar output — the network loses part of the damping capacity these devices would provide under normal operating conditions.

### On-Load Tap Changers (OLTC)

An OLTC (_On-Load Tap Changer_) is an electromechanical mechanism installed in large power transformers that adjusts the transformation ratio — and therefore the secondary output voltage — without interrupting energy flow. It regulates voltage against slow load variations, typically over a range of $\pm 10\%$ in discrete steps. Its characteristic response time, constrained by the mechanical inertia of the motor and gears, is on the order of several seconds per step.

### Per unit system (p.u.)

_Per unit_ (p.u.) is a dimensionless notation used in power systems to express any quantity (voltage, current, power, impedance) as a fraction of a reference (base) value. By convention, 1.0 p.u. equals the nominal value of the equipment or node: a voltage of 1.2 p.u. on a 220 kV network therefore corresponds to 264 kV.

### Positive feedback loop

A positive _feedback loop_ describes a mechanism in which an initial disturbance triggers a system response that, rather than opposing the deviation, amplifies it. In the context of the incident, each IBR plant trip reduced reactive absorption, which raised the voltage, which in turn triggered further trips: the system response reinforced the disturbance instead of damping it.

### Rate of Change of Frequency (RoCoF)

RoCoF (_Rate of Change of Frequency_) is the time derivative of system frequency, $df/dt$. It measures how quickly frequency deviates from its nominal value (50 Hz) in response to an active power imbalance. In high-inertia systems, RoCoF is moderate; in low-inertia systems, the same power imbalances produce much higher rates of change, compromising the coordinated operation of protection systems.

## Chapter 4 — Operational Response and Restoration

### Operating Procedure 1.6 (P.O. 1.6)

Operating Procedure 1.6 is the emergency protocol of the Spanish power system that establishes safeguard and supply restoration plans for critical incidents. It dictates strategies for topological fragmentation of the network into independent electrical islands, preferred energisation routes, and the prioritisation protocol for starting generation facilities to restore the system after a partial or total voltage blackout.

### Black Start

_Black Start_ capability is the ancillary service by which certain generation facilities can start up and begin injecting energy into the grid without requiring external electrical voltage. This capability, traditionally provided by hydroelectric plants with mechanical opening valves or thermal units with emergency diesel generators, is the primary requirement for initiating re-energisation (the _Bottom-Up_ strategy) of a power system after total collapse.

### Short-circuit power ($S_{sc}$)

Short-circuit power ($S_{sc}$) at a network node is a measure of its electromagnetic «strength». It represents the apparent current that would flow into that node in the event of a three-phase bolted fault. High short-circuit power, typically provided by large synchronous generators, implies that voltage at that node is highly robust and resilient, experiencing minimal variations under disturbances, sudden load connections, or network switching.

### Synchro-check relays

The _synchro-check_ relay (ANSI function 25) is a protection device used in coupling manoeuvres between separated electrical systems (islands). Its function is to continuously monitor that the voltage, frequency and phase angle on both sides of an open circuit breaker are within pre-set tolerance margins. Only when these three variables align does the relay authorise closing the breaker, ensuring islands synchronise safely without causing a severe transient impact.

### Regional Coordination Centres (RCC)

RCCs (_Regional Coordination Centres_) are supranational entities established by European regulations to facilitate operational cooperation between different Transmission System Operators (TSOs). Their role is not to operate breakers in real time, but to perform coordinated security analyses (CSA), calculate cross-border exchange capacities, and propose preventive and corrective actions to ensure N-1 compliance at regional level (e.g. Coreso for the South-West Europe region).

### Automatic Frequency Restoration Reserve (aFRR)

The aFRR (_automatic Frequency Restoration Reserve_), historically known as secondary regulation, is a system ancillary service that activates automatically following a frequency deviation. It is controlled directly by the System Operator's AGC (_Automatic Generation Control_) and its objective is to progressively return frequency to its nominal value (50 Hz) and restore interconnection flows to their scheduled values, thereby releasing the primary reserve (FCR) previously activated.

### Renewable Energy Control Centre (CECRE)

CECRE is the supervisory and control centre of Red Eléctrica de España, a world pioneer, responsible for monitoring and governing renewable energy generation (RCR regime) injection. Its function is to ensure that the massive integration of intermittent generation sources (wind and solar) takes place safely, with the authority to issue power reduction setpoints (_curtailment_) when system security requires it.

## Chapter 5 — Analysis of Technical Reports

### N-1 Security Criterion

The _N-1 Security Criterion_ is the fundamental safety standard in the operation and planning of electric power systems. It states that the system must be able to maintain voltage and frequency parameters within normative operational limits following the contingent loss of any single element (a generator, transmission line or transformer), without causing cascading supply interruptions or equipment damage.

### Renewables, Cogeneration and Waste Regime (RCR)

The Renewables, Cogeneration and Waste Regime (RCR) is the Spanish power system regulatory framework grouping electricity production facilities from decarbonised sources. Historically, the technical operating obligations imposed on this regime (such as voltage control or ancillary service provision) have differed substantially from those required of conventional synchronous generators.

### Operating Procedure 7.4 (P.O. 7.4)

P.O. 7.4 is the Spanish power system technical regulation governing the voltage control ancillary service on the transmission network. It defines generators' obligations to absorb or inject reactive power ($Q$) in response to setpoints issued by the System Operator, with the aim of keeping voltage profiles ($V$) within the safety ranges stipulated in P.O. 1.1.

### Static Synchronous Compensator (STATCOM)

A STATCOM (_Static Synchronous Compensator_) is an active reactive power compensation device based on power electronics (VSC inverters). Unlike discrete-switching mechanical reactors or capacitor banks, a STATCOM injects or absorbs reactive power continuously, dynamically and almost instantaneously, providing far superior voltage control against fast transients.

### Network Code on Requirements for Generators (NC RfG)

The _Network Code on Requirements for Generators_ is the European network code established by ENTSO-E that harmonises the mandatory technical requirements that generation facilities must meet to connect to the grid. Its objective is to ensure that all generators, regardless of synchronous or asynchronous technology, possess the resilience capabilities needed to maintain continental synchronous area stability.

### Synthetic inertia

_Synthetic inertia_ (or virtual inertia) is the ability of an inverter-based generation facility (IBR) to emulate the natural inertial response of a traditional synchronous machine. Through advanced control algorithms, the inverter injects or absorbs active power at ultra-fast rates proportional to the time derivative of frequency (RoCoF), mitigating sharp frequency variations under power imbalances.

### Grid-forming vs. Grid-following control

This concept defines the control paradigm of inverters. A _grid-following_ inverter synchronises passively with the pre-existing voltage and frequency, depending on an external network to operate. By contrast, a _grid-forming_ inverter acts as an ideal voltage source behind an impedance: it actively establishes its own voltage and frequency waveform, allowing autonomous network support, robustness provision (short-circuit power) and operation even with 100 % power electronics penetration or in island mode.

### Out-of-Step Tripping (OST)

OST (_Out-of-Step Tripping_) relays are systemic protection schemes designed to detect severe angular divergences between interconnected areas (pole slipping). When the phase angle difference exceeds electromechanical stability limits and power transfer becomes unsustainable, the relays automatically open interconnection lines to prevent structural damage and contain the collapse, preventing its propagation to neighbouring systems.

## Chapter 6 — Crisis Communication

### Crisis communication failure

In emergency management, a _crisis communication failure_ describes the institutional failure to occupy the information space promptly with verifiable messages following a serious incident. According to the _Chaos Communication Model_, if the responsible institution does not issue a clear account during the critical initial window (1–6 hours), the discursive vacuum is filled by alternative or unverified narratives that, once consolidated, are nearly impossible to reverse through subsequent technical corrections.

### Infodemic

Term popularised by the WHO to describe the overpopulation of the information space with unverified, erroneous or false content that spreads rapidly in crisis situations. In critical infrastructure incidents, the infodemic accelerates due to the asymmetry between the immediacy of rumour creation and the slowness of technical data verification, worsening when the very nature of the event (e.g. a blackout) deprives the population of normal access to reliable information sources.

### Media framing and agenda-shifting

_Framing_ is the process by which media select and emphasise certain elements of an event to propose a specific causal interpretation, influencing public attribution of responsibility. Related to this, _agenda-shifting_ occurs when a disruptive event is instrumentalised by media to divert attention and reopen pre-existing political or structural debates (such as the energy model or nuclear closures) that were not caused by the incident itself.

### Vacuum filling

_Vacuum filling_ is the structural and inevitable process by which collective uncertainty in the face of a disaster generates a demand for answers that, if not satisfied by official institutions, is spontaneously filled by unauthorised sources. According to SCCT theory (Coombs), causal explanations that fill this vacuum in the first hours tend to take deep root in public perception, shaping the crisis narrative in the long term.

### Emergent norm theory

The emergent norm theory (Turner and Killian) holds that, contrary to views of mass panic, groups in disruption situations spontaneously develop new rules of adaptive social behaviour. In the digital ecosystem during a blackout, this translates into the rapid creation of improvised mutual assistance networks, sharing of useful proximity information and the use of humour or irony as a collective mechanism to regulate emotional stress.

### Outrage communication

Based on Sandman's model (_Risk = Hazard + Outrage_), this concept indicates that public risk perception depends more on emotional factors (outrage, perception of negligence) than on technical assessment of the actual hazard. On social media, algorithms incentivise this pattern by viralising messages with high emotional charge and polarisation, disincentivising aseptic technical communication and shifting debate from physical causes to political blame attribution.

## Chapter 7 — Resilience and Future

### Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE)

The _Levelized Cost of Energy_ (LCOE) is the standard economic metric comparing the unit production cost of different technologies over their lifetime. Its main systemic limitation is that it ignores the value of ancillary services provided to the grid; thus, while IBR present an LCOE trending to zero, they do not natively provide the inertia or short-circuit power that traditional synchronous plants — with higher LCOE — implicitly contributed, giving rise to the paradox of firmness scarcity in highly renewable systems.

### Essential Reliability Services (ERS)

_Essential Reliability Services_ (ERS) group the physical attributes indispensable for safe network operation, such as inertia, short-circuit power, fast frequency response and dynamic voltage control. Since asynchronous technologies do not inherently provide these services as rotating machinery did, new regulatory frameworks (such as the P.O. 7.4 reform or NC RfG 2.0) require the creation of specific markets to explicitly define and remunerate their provision.

### Battery Energy Storage with Grid-Forming Inverters (BESS-GFM)

_Battery Energy Storage Systems with Grid-Forming inverters_ (BESS-GFM) combine high electrochemical density with power electronics capable of operating as an ideal, autonomous voltage source. Unlike _grid-following_ inverters, GFM topology allows the system to provide autonomous startup (_Black Start_), synthetic inertia, Fast Frequency Response (FFR) and active voltage support, thereby assuming the critical systemic stabilisation responsibilities in decarbonised networks.

### Fast Frequency Response (FFR)

_Fast Frequency Response_ (FFR) is a sub-cycle stabilisation service, designed for power electronics systems, that injects a massive block of active power within the critical time window (typically under 0.25 s) before the activation of traditional mechanical regulators. By drastically mitigating the rate of frequency fall (RoCoF), FFR effectively compensates for the lack of rotational inertia, having demonstrated in pioneer markets like ERCOT its capacity to reduce critical system security thresholds.

### Brownfield strategy

In energy infrastructure engineering, the _Brownfield_ strategy consists of repurposing existing industrial facilities — such as decommissioned thermal or nuclear plants — to provide new systemic functions. In this context, it involves retaining the original large alternators operating at no-load as synchronous condensers, providing natural inertia and short-circuit power, and leveraging already-built substations and evacuation lines to dramatically reduce capital costs and implementation timescales.

### EirGrid DS3 Programme

The _Delivering a Secure, Sustainable Electricity System_ (DS3) programme is EirGrid's (Ireland) pioneering ancillary services framework, designed to operate the island system with asynchronous renewable penetrations of up to 75 %. The programme fragments and remunerates stability through 14 highly specialised technical products — such as Synchronous Inertial Response (SIR) indexed to the technical minimum, or FFR with rebound penalties — setting the international standard for translating the physical needs of a decarbonised grid into economic incentives.

### Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G)

_Vehicle-to-Grid_ (V2G) technology enables the bidirectionality of electric vehicle batteries, allowing them to inject active and reactive power into the grid. Under the new European NC RfG 2.0 framework, charging infrastructures aggregating more than 1 MW of combined capacity will be classified as significant Storage Modules (Type B), imposing the obligation to incorporate _Grid-Forming_ capabilities to transform these mobility mega-parks into active dynamic stabilisation nodes.

### Under-Frequency Load Shedding (UFLS)

_Under-Frequency Load Shedding_ (UFLS) is the system defence's last-resort mechanism: when frequency falls below pre-defined thresholds (typically stepped between 49.0 Hz and 48.0 Hz in the Continental European Synchronous Area), UFLS relays automatically disconnect loads to restore the generation-demand balance. Its intrinsic limitation is actuation speed: high-voltage mechanical circuit breakers require between 60 ms and 100 ms to open, making them inoperable as a first barrier against disturbances with RoCoF above 2 Hz/s. In low-inertia systems ($H &lt; 3$ s), the 49 Hz threshold can be reached in under 200 ms, compressing the actuation window to the point of mechanical infeasibility.

### Grid-Following vs. Grid-Forming: inverter control architectures

The _Grid-Following_ (GFL) topology models the inverter as a controlled current source dependent on an external network voltage measurement (via the Phase-Locked Loop, PLL). Its advantage is simplicity and low cost; its critical limitation is that it cannot operate autonomously or establish voltage in weak networks. The _Grid-Forming_ (GFM) topology models the inverter as an ideal voltage source behind a virtual reactance, enabling autonomous operation, robust fault current injection and synthetic inertia. NC RfG 2.0 mandates the transition to GFM as compulsory for significant new installations.

### Synthetic Inertia

_Synthetic inertia_ is a control algorithm implemented in GFM inverters that mathematically emulates the behaviour of the swing equation of an electromechanical rotor. The algorithm continuously measures the time derivative of frequency ($df/dt$) and adjusts injected power proportionally, releasing or absorbing energy from the battery bank within tens of milliseconds. This sub-cycle response slows the rate of frequency decline, extending the time margin for conventional regulation systems to act, and is functionally equivalent to the kinetic mass of traditional synchronous machines but with much faster response.

### Short-Circuit Power

_Short-Circuit Power_ ($S_{sc}$) at a node is the instantaneous magnitude of current the system can inject in response to a voltage fault. It defines the electrical stiffness of the node: high $S_{sc}$ allows distance protection to operate correctly, overcurrent protection to coordinate selectively, and inverters to maintain synchronism of their control algorithms. GFM inverters can contribute to $S_{sc}$ through controlled fault current injection; however, their thermal limit (1.1–1.2 p.u.) is much lower than that of synchronous machines (5–7 p.u.), requiring the complement of synchronous condensers in weak networks.

### Synchronous Condensers (SynCons)

_Synchronous Condensers_ are synchronous rotating machines operated at no-load — without a prime mover — that provide genuine rotational inertia and fault current injection capability of 300–400 % of their nominal value. Their role in the transition to IBR-dominated systems is to provide nodal stiffness (high $S_{sc}$) and natural kinetic mass, complementing the speed and precision of GFM inverters. The _Brownfield_ strategy leverages alternators from decommissioned plants, dramatically reducing capital costs.

### Duck Curve

The _duck curve_ describes the daily profile of net regulation demand in systems with high solar penetration: a deep depression during the central hours of the day (when base consumption is low but solar generation is at its peak) followed by a steep evening ramp. Spring is the period of maximum depth and vulnerability. In the case of the 28 April event, the valley depth coincided with an extraordinarily sharp solar injection ramp, leaving the system with minimal reactive absorption capacity at the critical moment.

### VOLTAIRE system

The VOLTAIRE system (integrated into the Renewable Energy Control Centre, CECRE) is the architecture deployed by REE for dynamic voltage regulation in the peninsular system. It operates in two hierarchical layers: Tertiary Regulation globally optimises the national voltage profile through reactive optimal power flows; Secondary Regulation sends telematic setpoints in real time to renewable park inverters with a maximum sampling resolution of 4 s, closing a proportional-integral control loop that keeps voltage within fixed margins. Service remuneration follows the dynamic formula indexed to PMD established in the June 2025 revision of P.O. 7.4.

### Renewable Energy Control Centre (CECRE) — voltage control

The _Renewable Energy Control Centre_ (CECRE) is REE's operational entity responsible for real-time monitoring and dispatch of renewable parks and storage systems, as well as the execution of voltage control algorithms through the VOLTAIRE system. It integrates high-resolution telemetry (PMU) from significant facilities and executes sub-hourly auctions of ancillary services.

### Power System Stabilizers and Power Oscillation Damping (PSS/POD)

_Power System Stabilizers_ (PSS) and _Power Oscillation Damping_ (POD) systems are additional control modules installed in inverters (especially in GFM mode) that inject counter-phase signals designed to damp small- and large-perturbation electromechanical oscillations. They detect frequency or voltage oscillations and produce synchronised power injections or absorptions that cancel oscillatory amplitude, preventing the sub-synchronous oscillations (SSO) observed during the 28 April collapse. Royal Decree 997/2025 makes them mandatory in newly installed GFM inverters with power above 10 MW.

### Headroom: Inverter Capacity Reserve

_Headroom_ is the fraction of maximum apparent capacity ($S_{\max}$) that a GFM inverter must keep reserved without using it for active power injection in steady state. This reserve is necessary to ensure the inverter has sufficient margin to respond to fast voltage or frequency disturbances. Requiring headroom reduces energy market revenues, which constitutes the structural economic friction that justifies the creation of Essential Reliability Services (ERS) markets to explicitly remunerate this response capacity.

### NC RfG 2.0

The _Network Code on Requirements for Generators_ (NC RfG), now in its version 2.0, is the European technical standard establishing requirements that new generation and storage facilities must meet to connect to the grid. Version 2.0, proposed following the Iberian collapse, introduces the mandatory requirement for _grid-forming_ capabilities in significant new installations (Types B, C, D), requiring technology to enable synthetic inertia, dynamic voltage support and robust short-circuit current injection.

### Low Voltage Ride Through (LVRT)

_Low Voltage Ride Through_ (LVRT) is an inverter's ability to maintain energy injection during a voltage dip rather than disconnecting via protection. LVRT requirements in Spain are regulated by P.O. 12.3 and include the dynamic parameter $k$ (proportionality factor of reactive current relative to dip depth). The 28 April blackout demonstrated that in networks with $\mathrm{SCR} &lt; 2$, massive reactive injection according to traditional LVRT profiles can amplify instability rather than contain it, requiring revision of the coordination between LVRT control and network weakness.
