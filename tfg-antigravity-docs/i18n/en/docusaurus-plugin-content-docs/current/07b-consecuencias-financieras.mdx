<GlitchTitle>Financial Consequences and Resilience Costs: Economic Audit</GlitchTitle>

The systemic collapse of the Iberian Peninsula's electrical grid, which occurred on April 28, 2025, at 12:33 p.m. (CEST), represents one of the most severe critical infrastructure disruption events in the contemporary economic history of the European Union. With an instantaneous loss of **31 gigawatts (GW)** of load, the abrupt disconnection of Spain and Portugal's systems from the continental synchronous grid, and the direct impact on over **50 million citizens** and the entire peninsular productive fabric, the ramifications of this event transcend purely electromechanical phenomenology.

This event, classified by the European Network of Transmission System Operators for Electricity (ENTSO-E) as unprecedented and multifactorial, has exposed deep structural vulnerabilities in the design of the energy market, the planning of the ecological transition, and regulatory contingency schemes.

This technical report presents a comprehensive financial and macroeconomic audit, structured to unravel and quantify the direct repercussions of the blackout, the subsequent wave of litigation and sanction proceedings, and the unavoidable cost of structural technological adaptation (CAPEX) required to mitigate the risk of future tail-risk events.

Through the lens of energy economics and critical infrastructure auditing, the fundamental analytical premise governing this document axiomatically demonstrates that **the cost of capital allocated to systemic resilience is orders of magnitude lower than the massive value destruction** (contingent OPEX, lost profits, and cross-indemnifications) generated by grid vulnerability.

## 1. The Direct Cost of the Blackout (Immediate Losses and Macroeconomics)

A rigorous assessment of the immediate economic losses resulting from an electrical collapse cannot be restricted solely to the unbilled revenue of generation and distribution companies. On the contrary, it requires a general equilibrium analysis that parameterizes the paralyzing impact on the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) of the affected region during the period of widespread interruption. To articulate this calculation, the orthodoxy of energy economics mandates the use of the **Value of Lost Load (VoLL)** metric.

### Analysis of the Value of Lost Load (VoLL) in the Iberian Economy

VoLL constitutes the regulatory and academic standard —endorsed by the Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER) and the Council of European Energy Regulators (CEER)— for estimating the maximum marginal price consumers would be willing to pay (Willingness to Pay - WTP) to avoid a supply interruption, or alternatively, the compensation demanded (Willingness to Accept - WTA) for suffering such an interruption. It functions as a direct monetary approximation of the marginal consumer surplus associated with the continuity and security of the electrical infrastructure.

During the blackout of April 28, 2025, the Iberian Peninsula faced an absolute voltage collapse that suddenly disconnected 25.2 GW of base and peak load. The restoration of supply operated under an asymmetric and gradual dynamic; while 35% of demand had been restored by 7:00 p.m. on the same day, vast industrial and metropolitan areas did not regain operational normality until the early morning of April 29, with some nodes experiencing up to **14 hours of uninterrupted blackout** and technical work concluding at 2:36 p.m. on the 29th.

To parameterize the aggregate macroeconomic cost, it is imperative to disaggregate VoLL by sectoral segments, given the profound heterogeneity in energy dependence, process resilience, and the elasticity of financial damage relative to interruption time. The econometric methodologies of the European Union and Blackout Studies establish the following value ranges for developed economies equivalent to those of Spain and Portugal.

Taking into account the actual, scheduled, and forecast demand curve for April 28, and calculating the integral of the area under the curve from 12:33 p.m. (moment of collapse) until the progressive restoration of 99.95% of demand at 7:00 a.m. on April 29, the aggregate Energy Not Supplied (ENS) for the Iberian system conservatively falls within the range of **150,000 to 180,000 Megawatt-hours (MWh)**, i.e., between 150 and 180 GWh. *(Methodological note: The 150–180 GWh range reflects uncertainty in estimating elastic consumption destroyed versus displaced. The lower bound assumes partial recovery of nighttime industrial load; the upper bound assumes net destruction due to cold process shutdowns)*.

Applying a weighted average VoLL —which internalizes the concurrent paralysis of the primary, secondary, and tertiary sectors on a working day (Monday)— projects a devastating financial impact. Audits and preliminary estimates prepared by corporations such as CEOE and ATA converged on placing the direct and indirect value destruction in the range of **1,000 to 1,500 million euros**. This figure, consistent with the VoLL analytical model, reveals that the temporary lack of a good whose daily market price was at depressed levels (€18.50/MWh in the pool before the blackout) caused multi-million euro negative externalities.

This macroeconomic impact was systemically amplified by the collateral collapse of telecommunications and internet services. Global routing metrics showed that, at the instant of the blackout, internet traffic in mainland Portugal initially plummeted by half, falling by up to 90% in the subsequent five hours relative to the historical baseline, dragging down financial ecosystems, fintech platforms, and transnational cloud operations, generating intangible lost profits of massive proportions.

### Price Distortion in the Wholesale Market (OMIE) and the PVPC

The infrastructure crisis caused an immediate financial schism in the daily and intraday market managed by the Iberian Energy Market Operator (OMIE). In the temporal phase immediately preceding the collapse, specifically on the morning of April 28, the market showed clear symptoms of renewable oversupply; the marginal price was at abnormally low levels, averaging just **5.79 euros per megawatt hour (MWh)**, and even recording hourly periods with zero or marginally negative prices (where the system paid up to €10/MWh for energy withdrawal).

However, the topological reconstruction of the grid after the blackout (black-start) and the imperative need to inject rotational inertia and voltage control forced the system operator (REE) to drastically alter the hierarchy of economic dispatch. The restoration required partially sidelining renewable and hydroelectric generation, urgently resorting to natural gas combined cycle thermal plants. With gas dictating the marginal clearing price, the cost of electricity in the wholesale market experienced an exponential increase, multiplying by five almost instantaneously compared to pre-blackout levels.

This price increase did not operate in a vacuum but was passed on with great speed to consumers, especially those indexed to the Voluntary Price for the Small Consumer (PVPC). The extra costs arising from the technical restrictions necessary to keep the system afloat after the blackout caused regulated customers to experience a **125% increase** in the specific restrictions component of their bill, materializing in an average structural increase of over 3 euros per month per household and transferring an inefficient systemic cost to vulnerable consumer strata.

## 2. The Impact of Automatic Load Shedding on Electro-intensive Consumers

Beyond the commercial and residential sectors, the epicenter of direct material damage fell upon heavy industry. Faced with the uncontrolled drop in frequency and voltage, grid automation activated extreme survival protocols, triggering automatic load shedding. This emergency sectionalizing mechanism, designed to isolate nodes and prevent damage to the extra-high voltage transmission grid (400 kV), operated as an operational guillotine for industry.

Electro-intensive consumers, characterized by maintaining critical thermodynamic processes in continuous uninterrupted flow (steelmaking, metallurgy, electrolysis, refining), suffered lethal operational disruptions. The Association of Large Energy Consumption Companies (AEGE), which brings together giants like Acerinox, ArcelorMittal, Asturiana de Zinc (Azsa), Ercros, and Grupo Gallardo Balboa, reported audited financial losses exceeding **25 million euros** resulting from this single event.

This sum reveals the fragility of industrial CAPEX against grid disturbances and is structured into two clearly differentiated loss vectors:

1.  **Direct Operational Damages (Estimated at over 13 million euros):** The sudden suppression of electrical supply without a controlled shutdown ramp caused massive thermal collapses. In metallurgical plants, the interruption in electric arc furnaces led to the solidification of liquid metals in crucibles and casting lines, destroying refractory linings. Reactivating these lines involved entire days dedicated exclusively to industrial cleaning, removal of hardened slag, and replacement of capital equipment damaged by the voltage transients generated at the moment of load shedding.
2.  **Lost Profits and Opportunity Costs (Estimated at 12 million euros):** Although the net supply interruption in certain industrial parks ranged between four and fourteen hours, the thermodynamic inertia of these processes is relentless. The startup, preheating, and recalibration of production lines meant, in practical terms, "losses of several days afterwards," annihilating the factories' ability to meet scheduled deliveries (Just-In-Time) and inducing penalties in international supply contracts.

From a higher macroeconomic perspective, the lack of a grid architecture capable of guaranteeing supply and the inability to establish agile compensations have drastically eroded the reputational capital of Spain and Portugal. The operational risk of suffering unscheduled shutdowns financially outweighs any hourly discount on the megawatt-hour, demonstrating that security of supply is the non-negotiable pillar of industrial policy.

## 3. The Legal and Regulatory Cost (Sanctions and Litigation)

The effects of the April 28 blackout extended beyond the resynchronization of alternators, resulting in a complex institutional and legal scenario. What ENTSO-E diagnosed as a fault originating from a technical failure that was "unprecedented, unforeseeable, and multifactorial," motivated various audits and cross-commercial lawsuits at the domestic level.

### Sanctioning Proceedings by the CNMC

Operating under its prerogatives as the ex-post regulatory compliance supervisory body, the Energy Instruction Directorate of the National Commission on Markets and Competition (CNMC) launched an unprecedented scrutiny campaign. Arguing the existence of an ecosystem of technical indiscipline among market actors, the CNMC deployed a forensic investigation that analyzed the operational telemetry of the two years prior to the event, culminating in the initiation of up to **66 sanctioning proceedings**.

These administrative procedures impacted the core of the electricity and infrastructure management oligopoly: Iberdrola, Endesa, Naturgy, Repsol, and the system operator itself, Red Eléctrica de España (REE). The main charges revolved around indications of continuous breaches in daily scheduling regulations, deviations in dynamic voltage control instructions, and deficient injection/absorption of reactive power.

Of singular legal and technical relevance was the opening of a proceeding classified as "serious" against Mercuria Sostenible, the managing entity of the Common Evacuation Infrastructure (ICE) located at the Huéneja substation in the province of Granada. This investigation placed the promotion of renewable energy at the center of the regulatory storm, as topological analyses of the blackout pointed to this substation as "ground zero," functioning as a massive collection busbar where the lack of overvoltage mitigation led to the first massive cascading disconnection of photovoltaic, wind, and solar thermal plants.

The punitive framework deployed by the CNMC is strictly anchored in the articles of Law 24/2013 on the Electricity Sector (LSE), revealing formidable financial contingencies for the utilities' balance sheets:
- **"Serious" Infractions:** Typified under articles 64.15, 64.16, and 64.17 of the LSE, they carry pecuniary sanctions with a maximum ceiling of **6 million euros** per proceeding.
- **"Very Serious" Infractions:** Based on precepts such as article 64.37 and article 61.a (manifestly jeopardizing the guarantee of supply). The financial penalty stipulated for these offenses rises to a maximum limit of **60 million euros** per infraction.

### Commercial Litigation for Damages and Unfair Competition

In parallel to the administrative actions, the need to assign responsibilities led to litigation between large corporations, highlighting the economic weight of "reputational risk" in the energy market. A paradigmatic case has been the filing of a civil lawsuit by the Iberdrola group against the transmission grid operator Red Eléctrica (REE) and its parent company, Redeia Corporación, based on the Unfair Competition Law.

The genesis of this litigation lies in the statements made by Redeia's executive leadership during the crisis period, where they argued that REE executed "5,000 impeccable maneuvers" and shifted responsibilities towards the anomalous behavior of the renewable generation grid and self-consumption, directly pointing to critical inefficiencies in the voltage control of the large Núñez de Balboa photovoltaic plant, owned by Iberdrola.

Iberdrola structured a bold legal strategy, avoiding the contentious-administrative route, to bring the conflict into ordinary civil jurisdiction. Iberdrola forcefully argued that the public statements emanating from REE's top management were inaccurate, biased, and unnecessarily disparaging, constructing a media narrative that unfairly transferred responsibility for the national blackout to the private management of the utility, devaluing the company's prestige before its shareholders and temporarily penalizing its stock market value.

## 4. The Cost of Mandatory Reforms (CAPEX: Retrofitting and New P.O. 7.4)

The forensic analysis of the Iberian collapse incontrovertibly exposed the technical obsolescence of a transmission grid that has rapidly transitioned towards a high penetration of distributed power electronics incapable, under previous regulations, of sustaining critical parameters such as voltage and pure rotational inertia.

### Financial Sustainability and the "Reinforced Operation" (The Toxic OPEX)

As an emergency containment patch immediately following the blackout, Red Eléctrica established the so-called **"Reinforced Operation."** This operational doctrine severely restricts the participation of renewable energies in the daily market, forcing the centralized and massive scheduling of synchronous generation plants. Thus, the system went from having around 7 gas combined cycle plants activated under normal conditions to maintaining between 25 and 30 plants constantly burning natural gas to provide firmness, inertia, and short-circuit capacity.

From a management accounting perspective, the Reinforced Operation has become a financial black hole. Redeia's own corporate estimates put the direct cost of this extra effort at **711 million euros** for the twelve months following the incident alone. However, external audits conducted by global consultancies like PwC scaled up the real magnitude of the systemic damage, estimating an extra cost exceeding **1,000 million euros** compared to the 2024 financial year.

This sum of inefficiencies (OPEX) adds no patrimonial value to the grid; it is purely current expenditure to keep the system afloat. It worsens the trade deficit of Spain's balance of payments, drives up CO2 emission rights costs, and discourages investment by causing a massive increase in renewable curtailment.

### Retrofitting the Renewable Fleet and the New P.O. 7.4

The first pillar of the restructuring was formalized with the enactment of the new **Operating Procedure 7.4 (P.O. 7.4)**, entitled *"Ancillary service for voltage control of the transmission grid."*

This profound regulatory revision requires, retroactively and mandatorily, that special regime installations based on power electronics cease to operate as mere passive injectors of active power (P), assuming dynamic modulation obligations. The regulation compels these nodes to inject or absorb reactive power (Q) to sustain the voltage profile and respond to voltage dips (LVRT/HVRT).

To meet this requirement, developers are forced to invest capital in plant re-engineering: installing Plant Power Controller (PPC) architectures, adding static capacitor banks (STATCOMs), or physically oversizing modular inverter bays. Assuming half the fleet will require hardware modifications, and estimating a Unit Cost of upgrade between €5,000 and €15,000 per installed megawatt, **the aggregate retrofitting bill at the national level will range between 400 and 1,000 million euros**.

### Structural Investment: Synchronous Condensers and Large-Scale BESS Batteries

The reactive power control proposed by P.O. 7.4 is a necessary but insufficient condition. The ecosystem requires a second pillar of CAPEX: the strategic insertion of hybrid systems combining Large-Scale Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) with Synchronous Condensers at the transmission grid nodes with the highest congestion levels (Extremadura and Andalusia).

A synchronous condenser is a giant rotating generator coupled to the grid whose shaft spins in a vacuum. Its rotational mass provides pure synthetic inertia and an instinctive response capacity to short-circuit currents.

Based on real pilot projects (such as the 2026 corporate agreement between Engie and Rolwind in Andalusia for 278 MW of power and 1.1 GWh of storage), the parametric cost is approximately **0.86 Million Euros per MW of installed capacity**.

For the system operator (REE) to dispense with the gas over-scheduling that sustains the "Reinforced Operation," a minimum operational installation of between **3,000 and 4,000 MW** is required.

**Estimation of Structural CAPEX at the National Level:**

$$
3,500 \text{ MW} \times 0.86 \text{ M€/MW} \approx 3,010 \text{ Million Euros}
$$

Under the post-blackout tariff regime, the OPEX wasted on the cost of technical restrictions imposed by the "Reinforced Operation" amounts to over 1,000 million euros. In a basic financial evaluation model, a CAPEX of 3,000 million euros to provide the grid with clean inertia would have a dynamic payback period of less than five years. The Net Present Value (NPV) of the project is massively positive.

## 5. Contractual Conclusions: Resilience as a Profitable Investment

The forensic X-ray of the Great Blackout of April 28, 2025, transcends the strict nature of the electromechanical incident to form an event of macroeconomic schism. The aggregate accounting balance of the electrical catastrophe is undeniable:

- The cash flow squeeze of the electro-intensive sector (>€25M).
- The destruction of macroeconomic value from the lost profits of Energy Not Supplied (VoLL), estimated between **1,000 and 1,500 million euros**.
- The resulting legal warfare and coercive fines from the CNMC.
- The reactive OPEX exceeding **711 million euros annually** through the natural gas burning of the "Reinforced Operation."

Faced with the constant hemorrhage in the balance of payments of the electrical system, the investment effort for the comprehensive modernization of the ecosystem (between 3,000 and 3,500 M€ in CAPEX for Synchronous Condensers and P.O. 7.4 retrofitting) should not be conceptualized as a government liability, but as a strategic fiscal shield. Investing vast capital ex-ante in the physical and algorithmic robustness of the grid is not only the cheapest insurance imaginable but the absolute prerequisite to safeguard industrial sovereignty and ensure the irreversible success of the energy transition.
