<GlitchTitle>Financial Consequences and Resilience Costs</GlitchTitle>

Zero voltage has a price. What the chapter on resilience and the future describes in terms of grid physics — low inertia, capacitive saturation, absence of essential reliability services — has a direct translation in the profit and loss statement: lost production, damage to capital goods, market distortion, litigation in imbalance settlement, and a massive forced adaptation CAPEX. **Here, the economic vertex of the Energy Trilemma crystallizes:** the prior absence of ancillary service markets disincentivized the installation of grid-forming technologies, forcing the system to pay *ex post*, in the form of damages, for what it was unwilling to finance *ex ante* in the form of resilience. This chapter quantifies that translation across four fronts: the immediate macroeconomic impact, the sectoral damage to the electro-intensive industry, the open regulatory and judicial scenario, and the CAPEX required so that the Reinforced Operation is no longer essential.

The premise structuring the analysis is the same one underlying the Essential Reliability Services (ERS) markets discussed in the chapter on resilience and the future: investing in resilience before a collapse is orders of magnitude cheaper than financing its consequences afterward.

## 1. The direct cost of the blackout

### Energy Not Supplied and macroeconomic loss

The regulatory standard for quantifying the damage of an electrical interruption is the **Value of Lost Load (VoLL)**, endorsed by ACER and CEER. VoLL expresses how much a consumer would be willing to pay to avoid the outage — or what compensation they would demand for suffering it — and varies significantly depending on the affected sector and the duration of the interruption.

Integrating the real demand curve from 12:33 CEST on April 28 until the restoration of 99.95% at 07:05 on April 29, the **aggregate Energy Not Supplied (ENS)** for the Iberian system is estimated between <CuestionAbierta metricKey="reposicion.ens_espana_total">150 and 180 GWh</CuestionAbierta>. The lower limit assumes partial recovery of nighttime industrial load; the upper limit assumes net destruction due to cold shutdown of processes.

Applying a weighted average VoLL over the entire affected productive fabric — with the incident occurring on a working Monday and all three sectors active simultaneously — CEOE and ATA estimated in their preliminary audits a direct and indirect value destruction of between **1,000 and 1,500 million euros**. This figure is consistent with the model: a good that the day-ahead market valued at barely €18.50/MWh on the morning of April 28 generated, in its absence, multi-million euro negative externalities.

The impact was amplified by the parallel collapse of telecommunications. Internet traffic in mainland Portugal fell by 90% in the five hours following the zero voltage, dragging down banking systems, payment platforms, and transnational cloud operations. The lost profit of the digital economy, although difficult to quantify exactly, contributes to the upper range of the VoLL estimate.

### Price distortion in OMIE and impact on the PVPC

On the morning of April 28, the day-ahead market closed at €5.79/MWh, with periods of zero or negative prices due to renewable oversupply. The restoration abruptly reversed this equilibrium: the Black Start required displacing renewable and hydro generation and dispatching natural gas combined cycles as the marginal resource. The wholesale price multiplied by five almost immediately compared to pre-blackout levels.

The extra cost was passed on to consumers indexed to the **Voluntary Price for the Small Consumer (PVPC)**. The technical constraints necessary to sustain the system after the blackout caused a <CuestionAbierta metricKey="pvpc.incremento_restricciones_pct">125% increase</CuestionAbierta> in the constraints component of the regulated bill, equivalent to more than 3 euros per month per household. The systemic cost of grid fragility was, as usual, transferred to consumers most exposed to the regulated tariff.

## 2. Electro-intensive sector: direct damages and lost profits

The automatic load shedding — a defense mechanism designed to protect the transmission grid, not the industrial process — abruptly disconnected large industrial consumers without a shutdown ramp. The Association of High Energy Consumption Companies (AEGE), which includes Acerinox, ArcelorMittal, Asturiana de Zinc, Ercros, and Grupo Gallardo Balboa, documented audited losses exceeding **25 million euros**, structured along two vectors:

<img className="img-light-only" src="/figuras/aege_arc_furnace_light.png" alt="Electric arc furnace in electro-intensive industry during a supply interruption" />
<img className="img-dark-only" src="/figuras/aege_arc_furnace_dark.png" alt="Electric arc furnace in electro-intensive industry during a supply interruption" />

_Figure G3 — Operational damage in electro-intensive industry: interruption without a shutdown ramp in an electric arc furnace and risk of molten metal solidification. Synthetic illustration, own elaboration assisted by AI._

**Direct operational damages (>€13M).** The interruption without a shutdown ramp caused solidification of molten metals in electric arc furnaces, destroying refractory linings. Reactivation required full days of industrial cleaning, removal of solidified slag, and replacement of equipment damaged by the voltage transients generated at the moment of load shedding.

**Lost profits and contractual penalties (≈€12M).** The thermodynamic inertia of continuous processes — preheating, recalibration, quality verification — compromised scheduled deliveries for several days after the blackout. Penalties in international supply contracts, calculated on the price differential and delivery delay, complement the direct damage.

From an industrial policy perspective, the episode demonstrated that the operational risk premium associated with unstable supply exceeds in financial value any hourly discount in the megawatt-hour price. Security of supply is not a comfort attribute: it is a condition of viability for continuous process industry.

## 3. Sanctioning regime and litigation

### CNMC proceedings

The Energy Instruction Directorate of the CNMC initiated up to **66 sanctioning proceedings** against market operators, based on the analysis of operational telemetry from the two years prior to the event. Those affected include Iberdrola, Endesa, Naturgy, Repsol, and Red Eléctrica de España itself. The main charges refer to non-compliance with dynamic voltage control instructions and the deficient injection or absorption of reactive power required by the then-current P.O. 7.4.

Of particular technical relevance was the proceeding classified as **"serious"** against Mercuria Sostenible, manager of the Common Evacuation Infrastructure (ICE) at the Huéneja (Granada) substation. The topological analysis of the collapse identified this substation as the origin node of the first cascade of massive disconnections of photovoltaic, wind, and solar thermal plants, due to the absence of adequate overvoltage mitigation.

The punitive framework of Law 24/2013 on the Electricity Sector sets the following ceilings:

- **Serious** infringements (Art. 64.15, 64.16, and 64.17 LSE): up to **6 million euros** per proceeding.
- **Very serious** infringements (Art. 64.37 and Art. 61.a LSE): up to **60 million euros** per infringement.

The aggregate exposure of those under investigation, with 66 open proceedings, constitutes a regulatory contingency of an order of magnitude greater than any incident in the last decade in the Spanish electricity sector.

### Commercial litigation: Iberdrola vs. REE

Parallel to the administrative route, Iberdrola filed a civil lawsuit against Red Eléctrica and its parent company Redeia for unfair competition, framing the conflict in ordinary jurisdiction rather than administrative litigation. The basis of the action was the statements by Redeia's top management who, in defending the operator's "5,000 impeccable maneuvers," attributed responsibilities to the behavior of renewable generation — explicitly naming the management of the Núñez de Balboa photovoltaic plant, owned by Iberdrola. The lawsuit argues that these statements were inaccurate, unfairly transferred public responsibility to the private operator, and depreciated the company's stock market value.

The outcome of this litigation will set a precedent for the distribution of responsibilities in future systemic incidents, with implications that transcend the immediate parties.

## 4. The cost of reforms: toxic OPEX and necessary CAPEX

### The Reinforced Operation: a €1 billion per year patch

Immediately after the blackout, REE established the **Reinforced Operation**: an operational restriction that raises the minimum dispatch of synchronous generation from the usual approximately 7 combined cycles to between 25 and 30 permanently active units, to guarantee the firmness, inertia, and short-circuit power that the system needs and that the IBR fleet in grid-following mode cannot autonomously provide.

The cost of this measure is current expenditure without a counterpart in the grid:

- **>€666M** accumulated in the ten months from the blackout to March 2026 (verified by REE, April 2026). *(Note: Redeia's estimate for the full twelve months following the incident is €711M; external audits by PwC project an extra cost exceeding €1,000M compared to the 2024 financial year.)*
- Worsens the energy trade balance deficit due to increased burning of imported natural gas.
- Increases CO₂ emission rights in a grid that had reduced its carbon intensity by 75% since 2007.
- Causes growing renewable curtailment, disincentivizing new investment precisely in the technologies the system requires.

The Reinforced Operation is the most direct financial evidence of the incompatibility between the market design prior to April 28 and the technical reality of a grid with 82% IBR penetration. Each month of its duration reproduces the argument for ERS markets developed in Chapter 7: without explicit remuneration for stability services, the operator resorts to out-of-merit-order dispatch.

### Revised P.O. 7.4: retrofitting the existing fleet (€400–1,000M)

The CNMC Resolution of June 12, 2025 (BOE-A-2025-13076) requires that power electronics-based installations abandon the passive active power injection mode and assume dynamic voltage control obligations. The technical adaptation of plants that do not have grid-forming capability requires the installation of Plant Power Controllers (PPCs), STATCOMs, or oversizing of inverter bays. The regulatory framework, remuneration methodology, and timelines are developed in [Chapter 7 — §Revision of P.O. 7.4 and the VOLTAIRE system](./07-resilience-future.mdx).

The aggregate cost estimate, assuming half of the installed fleet requires hardware modifications with a unit cost of €5,000–15,000/MW:

$$
0.5 \times 130\,\text{GW} \times \overline{10{,}000}\,\text{€/MW} \approx 650\,\text{M€}
$$

The reasonable range — between **€400 and €1,000M** — reflects uncertainty in the percentage of the fleet that will require additional hardware versus firmware updates.

### Synchronous condensers and BESS: the structural CAPEX (≈€3,010M)

The retrofitting of P.O. 7.4 is a necessary but not sufficient condition to dispense with the Reinforced Operation. As demonstrated by the analysis in the chapter on resilience and the future, the causal mechanism of April 28 was not only the absence of dynamic reactive control: it was also the collapse of short-circuit power at nodes in the southern zone. This nodal stiffness can only be provided by an asset with real rotating mass or a grid-forming inverter backed by storage.

The deployment of hybrid BESS + Synchronous Condenser (SynCon) systems in the most vulnerable corridors — Extremadura and Andalusia — requires a minimum operational capacity of between 3,000 and 4,000 MW. With the parametric cost verified in the Engie-Rolwind agreement (Andalusia, 2026) for a 278 MW / 1.1 GWh system:

$$
3{,}500\,\text{MW} \times 0.86\,\text{M€/MW} \approx 3{,}010\,\text{M€}
$$

The financial argument for undertaking this investment is direct. With the Reinforced Operation costing over €1,000M per year in sterile OPEX, the simple payback period for the €3,010M CAPEX is less than three years. The Net Present Value of the project is positive even with conservative discount rates, and the investment also eliminates the CO₂ emissions penalty and renewable curtailment.

The reading of the balance sheet is unequivocal. The cost of not investing in resilience — measured solely in the OPEX of the Reinforced Operation, without including VoLL damage or litigation — exceeds in three years the total CAPEX necessary to structurally eliminate that dependence. April 28 demonstrated that the stability attributes that synchronous machines implicitly provided have a price: what was once a free externality now has a bill. That bill is paid in one of two ways: through CAPEX investment that provides them efficiently, or through the recurring OPEX of keeping the system afloat with natural gas. The second option, which is the current one, is the most expensive.
