## Theoretical Introduction

The **Swing Equation** is the fundamental model of transient stability in electrical power systems. It describes how the grid frequency changes in response to an imbalance between supplied power (P_m) and demanded power (P_e).

### Linearized Form (Frequency Domain)

For a real-time educational simulator, we use the linearized form of the Swing equation:

$$
2H \frac{d(\Delta f)}{dt} + D \Delta f = \Delta P
$$

**Where:**
- $H$ = Equivalent inertia constant of the system (seconds)
- $\Delta f$ = Frequency deviation in pu: $\Delta f = (f - 50) / 50$
- $D$ = Damping factor (pu)
- $\Delta P$ = Active power imbalance (% of the load)

### Physical Interpretation

- **$H$ (inertia)** determines the **initial rate** of frequency change (ROCOF). Lower H = higher ROCOF = faster collapse.
- **$D$ (damping)** determines the final **steady state**. Higher D = lower steady-state frequency drop (but only if primary reserves can respond).

**Critical point:** Inertia does not prevent collapse; it only delays it. Without available active power (sustained negative ΔP), the system will collapse regardless of H.

## Parameters Explained

### System Inertia (H)

The value of $H$ in seconds represents how long a synchronous machine could supply its rated power by extracting only its stored kinetic energy.

**Typical values:**

| Technology | H (seconds) |
|---|---|
| Large nuclear generator | 8–9 |
| Gas turbine (CCGT) | 6–7 |
| Hydroelectric | 3–4 |
| Wind turbine (Type 4, full converter) | 0 |
| Solar PV | 0 |
| Synchronous condenser | 1–2 |

**On April 28, 2025:**
- Iberian system with 78% renewables: **H = 2.45 seconds** (critical)
- Historical Iberian system (2015, more coal/nuclear): **H ≈ 5 seconds** (safe)

### Power Loss (ΔP)

Represents the percentage of the total load that is suddenly lost. On April 28, the loss of 2,200 MW in a 25,000 MW system = **8.8%**.

This is the "magnitude of the disturbance". Higher ΔP = higher ROCOF = faster collapse.

### Damping (D)

Represents two effects:
1. **Frequency-dependent load:** Induction motors consume less power at lower frequencies, providing "automatic relief"
2. **Automatic Voltage Regulator (AVR) response:** Synchronous machines automatically adjust their output in response to frequency changes

Typical values: **D = 1.5 pu** (baseline)

### Synthetic Inertia (FFR - Fast Frequency Response)

Modern inverters can be programmed to inject active power in proportion to the rate of change of frequency:

$$
P_{FFR} = -K_{FFR} \frac{df}{dt}
$$

This artificially mimics the inertial behavior of a synchronous machine. **But with a major caveat:** In weak grids (low SCR), high FFR gains cause control instability instead of helping.

### Weak Grid (SCR < 2.0)

**Short Circuit Ratio (SCR)** measures the "strength" of the grid:

$$
\text{SCR} = \frac{S_{cc}}{P_n}
$$

- **SCR > 3:** Strong grid (stable voltage)
- **2 < SCR < 3:** Medium grid
- **SCR < 2:** Weak grid (unstable voltage)
- **SCR < 1.5:** Very weak grid (PLL instability)

In southern Spain (Granada, Badajoz), the SCR was < 2.0 due to the very high concentration of solar power without synchronous support.

## The Three Key Pedagogical Interactions

### 1️⃣ **The Delay of Inertia**

**Action:** Load "28A Conditions" and observe the ROCOF ≈ 0.9 Hz/s. Then load "+ Inertia" (H=4.5s) and observe the same event.

**Observation:** The ROCOF is reduced to ≈ 0.6 Hz/s, but the frequency keeps dropping and reaches approximately the same minimum value.

**Lesson:** **Inertia is not a battery.** It does not prevent collapse; it only delays when it happens, buying time for primary reserves to respond. Without available power, collapse is inevitable.

---

### 2️⃣ **The Synthetic Inertia Trap in Weak Grids**

**Action:** Load "Synthetic FFR" with "Weak Grid" enabled. You will see that the initial ROCOF is reduced thanks to the FFR injection. But...

**Observation:** The frequency starts to **oscillate violently** at ~0.63 Hz, the subsynchronous range. The system becomes more unstable, not less.

**Lesson:** **Inverter control has limits.** In weak grids, PLLs cannot correctly track the voltage when the inverter itself is injecting power. This creates a feedback loop that causes the same 0.63 Hz instability observed in Granada before the blackout.

---

### 3️⃣ **The Limits of UFLS**

**Action:** Run "28A Conditions". You will see that UFLS triggers in stages (49.0 Hz, then 48.7 Hz, etc.) shedding load.

**Observation:** When ROCOF exceeds 1.0 Hz/s and the frequency drops below 48.0 Hz, even maximum UFLS does not stop the collapse.

**Lesson:** **UFLS is the last line of defense, but it has a breaking point.** Once inertia is so low that ROCOF > 1.0 Hz/s, synchronous machines trip due to "pole-slip" (loss of synchronism). At that point, UFLS is playing a losing game.

## Swing Equation: Full Form (Reference)

For scholars, here is the complete non-linear form:

$$
\frac{d^2 \delta}{dt^2} = \frac{\omega_s}{2H}(P_m - P_e(\delta) - D\frac{d\delta}{dt})
$$

Where $P_e(\delta) = \frac{EV}{X}\sin(\delta)$ is the electrical power as a function of the rotor angle $\delta$.

The simulator uses a linearized version that omits the angle and focuses directly on frequency, which is pedagogically more important.
