:::note Note on incident chronology
Throughout this chapter, reference is made to the five phases through which the National Security Council's Analysis Committee structured the incident: **Phase 0** (pre-incident days and morning of 28 April, latent voltage instability); **Phase 1** (12:00–12:30 CEST, electromechanical oscillations preceding the root trip); **Phase 2** (12:32:00–12:33:18 CEST, first generation losses from overvoltage); **Phase 3** (12:33:18–12:33:30 CEST, disconnection cascade to voltage collapse); **Phase 4** (from 12:33:30 CEST, progressive supply restoration). Detailed chronology is presented in Chapters 4 and 5.

## Generation fleet evolution: from synchronous machines to IBR-dominated mix

Despite more than a decade of structural stagnation in peak electricity demand in Spain, the generation fleet has undergone a profound transformation. Whereas in 2010 thermal and nuclear sources accounted for **65% of production**, by 2024 renewables had reversed that ratio, reaching **59% of the total mix**.

![Evolution of the generation mix in Spain (2010 vs. 2024)](/figuras/mix_comparativo_2010_2024.png) _Generation mix evolution in Spain: 2010 vs. 2024. Source: Peter Huber Centre / ESIOS._

By the first quarter of 2025, the peninsular system had consolidated this scenario, surpassing **100 GW of installed renewable capacity**. As of January 2025, almost 66% of total capacity came from decarbonised sources, with wind and solar PV in technical parity (24.9% each). Including distributed self-consumption, the global PV share exceeded **48.1 GW**, against **33.1 GW** of wind.

![Breakdown of installed generation capacity as of January 2025](/figuras/capacidad_instalada_2025.png) _Installed generation capacity in the Spanish system as of 31 January 2025. Source: NREL / Red Eléctrica._

As a direct counterpart, conventional synchronous generation has been progressively displaced: coal plays a residual role (**1.6%** of installed capacity), nuclear (**5.5%**) faces a progressive closure schedule, and more than **26 GW of combined-cycle gas turbines** (**20.4%**) operate at annual utilisation rates below 15%.

From a power systems engineering perspective, this transformation poses structural operational challenges. Historical network stability depended on synchronous generators — thermal, nuclear and hydraulic units — whose rotating masses provided immediate kinetic energy, <GlossaryLink term="Inercia (H)">physical inertia</GlossaryLink> and high fault currents. New solar and wind installations connect via <GlossaryLink term="IBR">inverters (_IBR_</GlossaryLink>), which operate in standard <GlossaryLink term="GFL (Grid-Following)">grid-following</GlossaryLink> mode: they synchronise their output to external voltage and frequency through <GlossaryLink term="Phase-Locked Loop (PLL)">PLL</GlossaryLink> algorithms and, lacking rotating masses, contribute neither synchronous inertia nor an intrinsic response to network fluctuations. The result is that the Iberian system routinely operates under conditions of low short-circuit capacity, high nodal impedance and amplified <GlossaryLink term="Tasa de Cambio de Frecuencia (RoCoF)">RoCoF</GlossaryLink> under any <GlossaryLink term="Potencia activa">active power</GlossaryLink> imbalance.

## Decarbonisation and operational implications

The environmental success of the Iberian energy transition is unambiguous. Sector emissions peaked at approximately **110 MtCO₂-eq** in 2007; by end- 2024, coinciding with a record renewable generation share (**56.8%**), they had fallen to **27.0 million tonnes of CO₂-eq** — a reduction of **75.7%** from the peak. The emission factor dropped from around 290 gCO₂/kWh in 2015 to approximately 80–100 gCO₂/kWh in 2024.

The operational counterpart is what this investigation examines: the substitution of thermal plants by inverter-connected renewable sources progressively withdraws the synchronous masses that provided inertia, dynamic <GlossaryLink term="Potencia reactiva">reactive power</GlossaryLink> and voltage control. The accelerated transition has led the Iberian system to routinely operate under low-inertia, low-nodal-strength conditions — whose relationship with the voltage instability of 28 April is analysed in Chapter 4.

## Pre-incident operational state: the abundance paradox

In the days preceding the blackout, the peninsular system operated under what technical literature has termed the **"abundance paradox"**. Unusually mild spring temperatures (~25 °C) and public holidays in several regions depressed the demand curve: at 12:30 CEST, minutes before the collapse, instantaneous demand stood at **25,184 MW** — **56%** of the system's historical peak demand.

This valley-demand scenario coincided with exceptional renewable resource availability, with non-synchronous generation covering **82% of the instantaneous mix**: approximately **18,000 MW** of solar PV (**53%**) and **3,500 MW** of wind (**11%**). To balance the active power surplus, the system simultaneously relied on hydraulic pumping (~3,000 MW) and net exports to France, Portugal and Morocco. Conventional synchronous generation was pushed to historical lows: nuclear at **10%** (3.4 GW) and gas combined cycles at a bare **3%** (~1,600 MW).

![Generation profile of the peninsular system on 28 April 2025](/figuras/ree_generation_mix_28april.png) _28 April generation profile: valley demand coincided with the solar PV peak, displacing synchronous generation to historical lows. Source: NREL / Red Eléctrica._

This reduction in rotating masses translated into exceptionally low synchronous inertia values: **1.3 s** in the Southern area and **1.84 s** in the Central area — both below the 2 s threshold recommended by <GlossaryLink term="ENTSO-E">ENTSO-E</GlossaryLink>. Sustained operation under these conditions had already produced dynamic stress signals on **16, 22 and 24 April**, with voltage instability episodes on the transmission network.

![Voltage oscillations at Núñez de Balboa substation, 22 April 2025](/figuras/precursor_overvoltage_22april.png) _Voltage oscillations recorded at Núñez de Balboa (400 kV) during the precursor event of 22 April. Several plants that tripped on 28 April had already experienced identical trips during this prior event. Source: IIT-ICAI / Compass Lexecon._

The most severe precursor occurred on **22 April at ~19:00 h**: an overvoltage spike exceeding **430 kV** triggered protection relays, disconnecting multiple PV and wind plants. The fact that several installations which tripped in the first seconds of 28 April had already experienced identical trips in this prior event is interpreted by the ICAI report as evidence that the system was operating with **structurally narrow dynamic voltage control margins**.

## Interconnection capacity: the Iberian energy island

### The structural island condition

The Iberian Peninsula operates as an **«energy island»** within the Continental European synchronous area. Its exchange capacity with France historically sits between **3% and 5%** of installed generation capacity — or **7.9%** relative to peak demand — well below the EU's binding **15% target for 2030**. This constraint conditions supply security in two ways: perturbations originating in the European inertia centre are amplified as they propagate to the Iberian periphery (**whip effect**), and in the event of a massive generation loss, the interconnection bottleneck physically limits the dynamic support importable from large central European alternators.

### Commercial NTC versus physical power flows

A critical distinction must be drawn between commercial limits and actual physical flows. Market exchanges are governed by the Net Transfer Capacity (NTC), agreed _ex ante_ between <GlossaryLink term="REE">REE</GlossaryLink> and RTE. However, in fast transient conditions, active and reactive power flows obey Kirchhoff's laws regardless of market schedules. Forcing a physical flow beyond the angular stability limit — typically a 90° angular difference between terminals — does not increase transferred power but instead causes pole slip and consequent loss of <GlossaryLink term="Sincronismo">synchronism</GlossaryLink>: the mechanism that materialised on 28 April at **12:33:21 CEST**.

![Deviation between commercial NTC schedule and physical flow at the ES-FR border](/figuras/entsoe_flow_deviation.png) _Deviation between the commercial exchange programme (NTC) and the actual physical power flow at the Spain–France border during the morning of 28 April. Source: ENTSO-E Factual Report._

### The HVDC INELFE-1 link and the critical control mode decision

The French interconnection combines AC overhead lines with the underground <GlossaryLink term="HVDC">HVDC VSC</GlossaryLink> INELFE-1 link (Santa Llogaia–Baixas, **2,000 MW** nominal). This link supports three operationally relevant modes: **PMODE1** (constant power, no dynamic grid response), **PMODE2** (power modulation by frequency deviation) and **PMODE3** (AC emulation, responds to angular difference between terminals).

During the pre-incident period, INELFE-1 was operating in **PMODE3**. However, at **12:08 CEST**, to damp the 0.6 Hz oscillations detected in the system, the mode was switched to **PMODE1**, fixing an export of **1,000 MW** towards France. The consequences were critical: at the moment of collapse, AC transpyrenean lines attempted to import up to **4,609 MW** of emergency power from France, while the HVDC link — locked in PMODE1 — continued extracting 1,000 MW from the Iberian system, lacking an active frequency control loop that could reverse the flow direction. Effective support from the French system was thereby reduced relative to the theoretical maximum available. At **12:33:21 CEST**, the AC lines tripped on loss of synchronism, fully isolating the peninsula from the continental synchronous system.

![Control mode transition in the HVDC INELFE-1 link](/figuras/hvdc_control_transition.png) _PMODE3 → PMODE1 transition at 12:08 CEST in the HVDC INELFE-1 link. The decision limited the link's dynamic response capability during the subsequent cascade. Source: IIT-ICAI / AELEC._

### Southern border: Morocco and black start capability

The Moroccan connection (submarine AC cables, **900 MW** technical capacity) was supplying **314 MW** of imports moments before the collapse. At **12:33:20 CEST**, the Moroccan operator's underfrequency relays tripped at the **49.5 Hz** threshold, disconnecting the link to protect its network from Iberian instability propagation. While protective for Morocco, this disconnection had important implications for restoration: voltage injection from the south provided the reference needed hours later to initiate <GlossaryLink term="Black Start">Black Start</GlossaryLink> procedures for Andalusian combined-cycle plants.

## The European awareness system (EAS) and the «false normality»

### Operational state classification (SO GL)

The <GlossaryLink term="SO GL (System Operation Guidelines)">System Operation Guidelines</GlossaryLink> establish five progressive severity levels: **Normal** (compliance with the <GlossaryLink term="Criterio N-1">*N*−1 Criterion</GlossaryLink>), **Alert** (degraded security margins requiring preventive action), **Emergency** (security limit violations or <GlossaryLink term="UFLS (Underfrequency Load Shedding)">UFLS</GlossaryLink> activation), **Blackout** and **Restoration**.

### The abrupt Normal → Blackout transition

Analysis of <GlossaryLink term="EAS (ENTSO-E Awareness System)">EAS</GlossaryLink> records reveals a critical dissonance between the actual dynamics of the Iberian network and its representation on the European platform. During the morning of 28 April, the system experienced at least two significant oscillations: a forced oscillation at **0.6 Hz** at **12:03 CEST** and an inter-area mode at **0.2 Hz** at **12:19 CEST**. Despite this, neither REE nor REN declared an Alert state. At **12:32:00 CEST**, the official status remained «Normal». Records confirm that the system transitioned abruptly from **Normal** to **Blackout** (criterion OB3) at **12:33:29 CEST**, without passing through any intermediate phase.

This failure to escalate generated limited visibility for neighbouring operators. The French operator (RTE) received no signals of dynamic vulnerability south of the Pyrenees; with the EAS remaining at «Normal», no cross-border cooperation protocols were activated and the French network was not prepared for the transient that reached the border at 12:33:21 CEST. The episode demonstrates that the EAS acted not as an early warning tool but as a **post-mortem notifier** of the collapse — a structural limitation of the European monitoring system's ability to capture fast voltage dynamics in low-inertia networks, and one of the central regulatory lessons of the incident.

The abrupt Normal → Blackout transition is captured in the simultaneous collapse of both variables. Source: ENTSO-E / REE._
