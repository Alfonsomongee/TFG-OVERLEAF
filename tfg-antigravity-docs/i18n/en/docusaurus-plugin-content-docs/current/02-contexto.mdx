## Evolution of the Generation Fleet: From Synchronous Generation to an IBR-Dominated Mix

Below, official ENTSO-E transparency data illustrates the current operational reality of the system compared to the scenario of April 28:

Although peak electricity demand in Spain has been structurally stagnant for over a decade, the generation fleet has undergone a profound transformation. While in 2010 thermal and nuclear sources represented 65% of production, by 2024 renewables had reversed that proportion, reaching 59% of the total mix.

In the first four months of 2025, the peninsular system consolidated this scenario, surpassing 100 GW of installed renewable capacity. As of January 2025, nearly 66% of total capacity came from decarbonized sources, with wind and solar photovoltaic energy at technical parity (24.9% each). Including distributed self-consumption, the global photovoltaic share amounted to over 48.1 GW, compared to 33.1 GW for wind.

Conversely, conventional synchronous generation has been progressively displaced: coal occupies a residual role (1.6%) of installed capacity, nuclear (5.5%) faces a progressive decommissioning schedule, and the more than 26 GW of combined cycle gas plants (*20.4%*) operate with annual utilization factors below 15%.

From a power systems engineering perspective, this transformation poses structural operational challenges. The historical stability of networks relied on synchronous generators — thermal, nuclear, and hydro — whose rotating masses provided immediate kinetic energy, physical inertia, and high short-circuit currents in the event of any disturbance. New solar and wind installations connect via inverters (IBR), which operate in standard mode as grid-following devices: they synchronize their export with external voltage and frequency through PLL algorithms and, lacking rotating masses, do not contribute synchronous inertia or intrinsically respond to grid fluctuations. This reduction in synchronous mass simultaneously affects two critical stability parameters: on one hand, inertia (associated with frequency and modeled by the Swing Equation); on the other, short-circuit power (associated with voltage stability). The result is that the Iberian system routinely operates with low short-circuit capacity, high nodal impedance, and amplified RoCoF in the face of any active power imbalance. The Systemic Vulnerability Radar is available in Annex IX · Methodology, Models, and Data.

<KeyFact id="inercia-28a">

The swing equation governs frequency behavior in response to active power disturbances. However, April 28 confirmed that the system had $H = 2.3$ s as a peninsular weighted average — above the ENTSO-E threshold of 2.0 s. Nevertheless, zonal values were significantly lower: 1.3 s in the south and 1.84 s in the center, both below the threshold. The collapse originated in the weakest zone, not the average — and it still collapsed. The relevant mechanism was of a different nature: the loss of grid strength measured by the SCR, which determined the voltage dynamics in the Q-V plane. This reading establishes the analytical hierarchy of the incident: first voltage stability, then inertia. The didactic model of the swing equation is available in Annex IX · Methodology, Models, and Data.

## Decarbonization and Operational Implications

The environmental success of the Iberian energy transition is indisputable. Electricity sector emissions reached their historical peak around 110 MtCO₂-eq in 2007; by the end of 2024, coinciding with the historical record for renewable generation (*56.8%*), they had fallen to 27.0 million tonnes of CO₂-eq — a 75.7% decrease from the peak. The emission factor dropped from 290 gCO₂/kWh in 2015 to values close to 80–100 gCO₂/kWh in 2024.

The operational counterpart is what this investigation examines: the replacement of thermal power plants with renewable sources connected via inverters entails the progressive withdrawal of the synchronous masses that provided inertia, dynamic reactive power, and voltage control. The Iberian system routinely operates under low inertia and reduced nodal robustness conditions — the relationship of which to the voltage instability of April 28 is analyzed in Chapter 3.

This configuration of low inertia with high IBR penetration is not exclusive to Iberia: the collapses in South Australia (2016) and the United Kingdom (2019) demonstrated analogous vulnerabilities whose regulatory lessons are examined in [Chapter 7](./07-resiliencia-futuro.mdx).

## Pre-Incident Operational State: The Paradox of Abundance

In the days leading up to the blackout, the peninsular system operated under what the technical literature has termed the "paradox of abundance." Unusually mild spring temperatures (approximately 25 °C) and the holiday calendar depressed the demand curve: at 12:30 CEST, just minutes before the collapse, instantaneous demand stood at 25,184 MW — 56% of the historical peak demand.

This low-demand scenario coincided with exceptional renewable resource availability, such that non-synchronous generation covered 82% of the instantaneous mix: approximately 18,000 MW of solar photovoltaic (*53%*) and 3,500 MW of wind (*11%*). To balance the active power surplus, the system simultaneously resorted to pumped hydro storage (approximately 3,000 MW) and net exports to France, Portugal, and Morocco. Conventional synchronous generation was displaced to historical lows: nuclear at 10% (3.4 GW) and combined cycle gas plants at a meager 3% (approximately 1,600 MW). The operational comparison with current data is reserved for the Real-Time ESIOS/ENTSO-E Data section.

This reduction in rotating masses translated into exceptionally low synchronous inertia values: 1.3 s in the Southern area and 1.84 s in the Central area — both below the 2 s threshold recommended by ENTSO-E. Prolonged operation under these conditions had already produced dynamic stress signals on April 16, 22, and 24, with episodes of voltage instability in the transmission network.

The most severe episode occurred on April 22 at approximately 19:00 h: an overvoltage peak exceeding 430 kV activated protections and disconnected multiple photovoltaic and wind plants. The fact that several of the installations that tripped in the first seconds of April 28 had already suffered identical trips in this prior event is interpreted by the ICAI report as evidence that the system was operating with structurally narrow dynamic voltage control margins. The oscillogram of the precursor episode is documented in Annex II · Dynamic Stability, Frequency, and Voltage.

### The Structural Island Condition

The Iberian Peninsula operates under the condition of an "energy island" within the synchronous area of Continental Europe. Its exchange capacity with France historically stands between 3% and 5% of installed generation capacity — or 7.9% measured against peak demand — well below the binding 15% target set by the EU for 2030. This limitation conditions supply security in two ways: disturbances originating in the European inertia center are amplified as they propagate towards the Iberian periphery (*whip effect*), and in the event of a massive generation loss, the interconnection bottleneck physically limits the dynamic support importable from large Central European alternators.

### Commercial NTC vs. Physical Flow

It is essential to distinguish between commercial limits and actual physical flows. Market exchanges are governed by Net Transfer Capacity (NTC), agreed ex-ante between REE and RTE. However, during fast transient regimes, active and reactive power flows obey Kirchhoff's Laws independently of the market schedule. Forcing a physical flow beyond the angular stability limit — typically a 90° difference between ends — does not increase transferred power but causes pole divergence and subsequent loss of synchronism: the mechanism that materialized on April 28 at 12:33:21 CEST. The NTC/physical flow deviation of April 28 is documented in Annex IV · Interconnections and Cross-Border Flows.

### The INELFE-1 HVDC Link and the Critical Control Mode Decision

The interconnection with France combines trans-Pyrenean AC lines with the underground HVDC VSC link INELFE-1 (Santa Llogaia–Baixas, 2,000 MW nominal). This link supports three relevant operating modes: *PMODE1* (constant power, no dynamic response to the grid), *PMODE2* (power modulation by frequency deviation), and *PMODE3* (AC emulation, responds to the angular difference between ends).

During the prelude to the blackout, INELFE-1 was operating in *PMODE3*. However, at 12:08 CEST, to dampen the detected 0.6 Hz oscillations, the mode was changed to *PMODE1*, fixing an export of 1,000 MW to France. The consequences were decisive: at the moment of collapse, the trans-Pyrenean AC lines attempted to import up to 4,609 MVAr (reactive power) of emergency support from France, while the HVDC link — fixed in PMODE1 — continued to extract 1,000 MW from the Iberian system due to the lack of a frequency control loop that could reverse the flow direction. Effective support from the French system was thus reduced relative to the theoretical maximum available. At 12:33:21 CEST, the AC lines opened due to loss of synchronism, completely isolating the peninsula from the continental system.

### Southern Border: Morocco and Black Start

The connection with Morocco (submarine AC cables, *900 MW* technical) provided 314 MW of imports moments before the collapse. At 12:33:20 CEST, the Moroccan operator's under-frequency relays tripped upon reaching the 49.5 Hz threshold, disconnecting the link to protect its grid. This disconnection, although protective for Morocco, had relevant consequences for restoration: the voltage injection from the south provided, hours later, the necessary reference to initiate Black Start strategies for the combined cycle plants in Andalusia.

## The European Alert System (EAS) and the "False Normalcy" {#eas-falsa-normalidad}

### Classification of Operational States (SO GL)

The ENTSO-E System Operation Guidelines establish five levels of progressive severity: *Normal* (compliance with the N−1 Criterion), Alert (degradation of margins requiring preventive actions), Emergency (violation of safety limits or activation of UFLS), Blackout, and Restoration.

### The Abrupt Normal → Blackout Transition

Analysis of EAS records reveals a critical dissonance between the actual dynamics of the Iberian grid and its representation on the European platform. During the morning of April 28, the system suffered at least two significant oscillations: a forced 0.6 Hz oscillation at 12:03 CEST and an inter-area mode of 0.2 Hz at 12:19 CEST. Despite this, neither REE nor REN declared the Alert state. At 12:32:00 CEST, the official status remained "Normal." Records confirm that the system transitioned abruptly from *Normal* to *Blackout* (OB3 criterion) at 12:33:29 CEST, without passing through any intermediate phase.

This lack of escalation generated limited visibility for neighboring operators. The French operator (RTE) received no signals of dynamic vulnerability south of the Pyrenees; with the EAS remaining in "Normal" state, cross-border cooperation protocols were not activated, nor was the French grid prepared for the transient that affected the border at 12:33:21 CEST. The episode highlights that the EAS acted not as an early warning tool, but as a post-mortem notifier of the collapse — a structural limitation of the European monitoring system for capturing fast voltage dynamics in low-inertia grids, and one of the central regulatory lessons of the incident.

## The Energy Transition Trilemma: The Analytical Thread

The April 28 blackout cannot be reduced to a simple hardware failure or an unfortunate sequence of relay trips. As will be detailed throughout this investigation, the incident was the physical manifestation of a structural mismatch affecting modern electrical systems, conceptualized as the **Energy Transition Trilemma**. This trilemma establishes that the transition must be simultaneously balanced across three vertices to be operationally sustainable:

1. **Technical Vertex:** The replacement of rotating masses (synchronous inertia and short-circuit power) with power electronics (grid-following inverters), which alters fundamental stability dynamics. (*Analyzed in Chapters 3 and 4*).
2. **Regulatory Vertex:** The obsolescence of Grid Codes, which do not require new IBR installations to withstand the degraded voltage conditions they themselves create by displacing conventional plants. (*Analyzed in Chapter 7*).
3. **Economic Vertex:** The absence of ancillary service markets that economically remunerate grid-forming inverters or synchronous compensators, disincentivizing their installation. (*Analyzed in Chapter 7b*).

Throughout the following chapters, this trilemma will serve as a cross-cutting framework for understanding why the collapse was, under the conditions of April 2025, structurally predictable.

Against this backdrop of maximum fragility — 82% non-synchronous generation, zonal inertia of 1.3 s, reduced grid strength, and EAS in Normal state — Chapter 3 reconstructs the 32 seconds in which the system went from equilibrium to total collapse.
