<GlitchTitle>Resilience and the Future</GlitchTitle>

The structural lesson of April 28 lies not in the collapse itself, but in the type of vulnerability it revealed. Forensic analysis demonstrated that the voltage zero was not a failure of active power reserve nor an isolated operational error: it was the terminal manifestation of a fundamental incompatibility between the physics of an inverter-dominated system and a technical-regulatory framework designed for synchronous grids. The scenario that enabled the April 28 event—high IBR penetration, scarcity of coupled synchronous generation, and depressed net demand—was not a climatic anomaly, but a preview of conditions that will recur with increasing frequency as decarbonization progresses.

## The Physics of Systemic Fragility

### Inertia, RoCoF, and the Swing Equation

The inertia constant $H$ determines the speed of frequency response to active power imbalances. Its reduction—a direct consequence of replacing rotating masses with grid-following inverters, which are decoupled from grid frequency via their DC links—shortens the time window available for regulation systems. The dynamics are governed by the Swing Equation:

$$
\frac{2H}{f_0} \, \frac{df}{dt} = P_m - P_e - D \cdot \Delta f
$$

where $H$ is the system's equivalent inertia constant (in seconds), $f_0$ the nominal frequency (50 Hz), $df/dt$ the RoCoF, $P_m$ the total mechanical power supplied to the coupled synchronous rotors, $P_e$ the generated electrical power, and $D$ the damping coefficient of frequency-dependent loads.

The system certified $H = 2.3$ s at the time of collapse<KeyFact id="inercia-28a" mode="callback">H = 2.3 s average / 1.3 s southern zone — Chapter 2</KeyFact>—above the 2.0 s threshold recommended by ENTSO-E: the April 28 event was not a frequency collapse due to a deficit of synchronous mass. However, the relevant dynamics were not frequency but voltage: the zonal values of $H$ in the south (1.3 s) and the degradation of SCR below 2 at critical nodes configured the capacitive instability conditions that [Chapter 3](./03-analisis-incidente.mdx) reconstructs. In scenarios with IBR penetration above 80%, both metrics—inertia and grid strength—degrade simultaneously, and it is the latter that determines vulnerability to ultra-fast voltage transients.

### Short-Circuit Degradation: The Grid Strength Metric

The dimension most directly linked to the causal mechanism of the April 28 event is the degradation of Short-Circuit Power ($S_{sc}$) at grid nodes. A conventional synchronous generator can inject short-circuit currents between 5 and 7 times its nominal value during the first cycles of a transient. Inverters limit injection to 1.1–1.2 p.u. to prevent semiconductor fusion.

The usual parameterization of this loss is the Short Circuit Ratio (SCR):

$$
\mathrm{SCR} = \frac{S_{sc,\mathrm{PCC}}}{P_{\mathrm{IBR}}}
$$

During the hours leading up to the collapse, large areas of the Iberian Peninsula were operating as very weak grids (SCR < 2): massive solar production had displaced combined cycle plants based on merit order, and their disconnection precisely eliminated the $S_{sc}$ sources that stiffen the nodes of the transmission grid. Under these conditions, with high $X/R$ but $\mathrm{SCR} \ll 2$, the overall impedance makes voltage sensitive to active power variations as well: solar injection ramps acquire a voltage disturbance power that would be harmless in conventional synchronous grids.

### The Geometric Paradox of Inverters: P-Q Conflict

There is an inherent structural contradiction in the geometry of the power triangle. The maximum apparent capacity of an inverter is bounded by:

$$
S_{\max} = \sqrt{P^2 + Q^2}
$$

When a photovoltaic plant experiences a sudden clearing of cloud cover, its MPPT algorithms command active power injection ramps $P$ of thousands of MW/h. To avoid exceeding $S_{\max}$, the controller must simultaneously reduce its reactive power margin $Q$. The paradox: this is precisely the moment when the sharp increase in $P$ on a grid with SCR < 2 causes severe overvoltage, demanding massive reactive power absorption from the inverter to contain it. The inverter faces two simultaneously incompatible physical obligations: maximize $P$ due to market signals and maximize $Q$ due to voltage stability needs.

### The Duck Curve and the Effect of Distributed Self-Consumption

The operational scenario of April 28 was situated in the deep valley of the duck curve: structurally low gross demand—analogous to the minimums of two decades ago—with irradiation close to summer maximums due to the lower thermal degradation in spring.

At 12:30 CEST, REE had exhausted its manual management tools: preventive opening of dozens of extra-high voltage lines to increase series impedance, plus operation of reactors at 85% of their capacity—without managing to contain the voltage rise.

An additional factor identified as relevant was distributed photovoltaic self-consumption: distributed generation subtracts visible demand without being telemetered at the necessary temporal resolution. During the preceding oscillatory phenomena, voltage fluctuations activated the anti-islanding relays of domestic and light industrial installations, which disconnected en masse, suddenly revealing the demand they were covering: the effect was equivalent to an unanticipated positive load step at a moment when the system already lacked the reactive margin to absorb it.

## International Precedents: Regulatory Lessons from Five Blackouts

A review of the five large-scale blackouts that chronologically preceded the April 28 event reveals a recurring pattern: the physical mechanisms of the Iberian collapse—OLTCs without hierarchical blocking, inverters without dynamic control, absence of mandatory minimum inertia—were documented and partially resolved in other jurisdictions before April 2025.

The cross-cutting lesson is twofold. First: post-incident regulatory measures, once implemented, demonstrate uniformly high effectiveness. Second: implementation takes between 1 and 11 years, during which the system remains exposed to the already documented vulnerability. The Iberian April 28 event fits this pattern: it reproduces already known causal mechanisms—particularly the destructive role of OLTCs (Italy 2003) and the fragility of inverters without dynamic control (S. Australia 2016, UK 2019)—whose integration into the peninsular regulatory framework was pending.

The technological solutions examined below constitute the industrial translation of these lessons.

## Enabling Emission-Free Technologies

Overcoming this vulnerability requires a transition in control architecture: from a generation fleet that *follows* voltage and frequency towards an infrastructure capable of *forming* them autonomously. Three complementary technology families articulate the response.

### BESS with Grid-Forming Control: Speed and Precision

Unlike a GFL inverter, a grid-forming (GFM) inverter operates as a Thévenin equivalent: it autonomously synthesizes an internal voltage reference in magnitude and angle ($V\angle\delta$) behind a virtual coupling impedance. By imposing this vector instantaneously, the GFM inverter inherently injects or absorbs active and reactive current in response to any grid variation, without relying on external phase measurements or calculation delays.

When the GFM inverter is backed by a battery bank (BESS-GFM), the combination provides two additional services:

- Synthetic inertia: the algorithm continuously measures $df/dt$ and adjusts output power proportionally within tens of milliseconds, emulating the behavior of a rotating mass.
- FFR: upon exceeding a RoCoF threshold or frequency deviation, the BESS injects a predefined block of active power sub-cyclically (≤ 0.25 s), halting the rate of decline before the speed governors of synchronous units have processed the disturbance.

Operational experience from ERCOT (Texas) quantifies the value of this service. The structural critical inertia threshold is around 100,000 MW·s: below this level, the RoCoF following a Category C contingency (≈ 2,750 MW) sinks the frequency before mechanical governors can act. The system operates with three alert zones: green (> 120,000 MW·s), yellow (110,000–119,999 MW·s), and red (< 100,000 MW·s, with forced dispatch of synchronous units out of merit order).

ERCOT's Responsive Reserve Service – Fast Frequency Response (RRS-FFR) market requires: autonomous detection of a drop below 59.85 Hz, injection of 100% of contracted power within a maximum of 0.25 s, and maintenance for a minimum of 15 minutes. Verified results show that integrating 450 MW under this scheme allowed reducing the critical inertia threshold from 100,000 MW·s to 88,000 MW·s—a 12% decrease reflecting the functional equivalence between the sub-second action of power electronics and the kinetic mass of synchronous machines.

The foundational global success story in this area is the Hornsdale Power Reserve in South Australia (operated by Neoen and Tesla). After its firmware upgrade to grid-forming in 2022, this 150 MW / 193.5 MWh BESS system certified the operational provision of 2,000 MWs of equivalent inertia. The Australian operator AEMO approved this contribution, which alone can cover approximately 15% of the minimum inertia required in the state, empirically demonstrating the viability of sustaining large-scale grids purely with power electronics.

However, IGBT semiconductors rarely sustain currents exceeding 1.2–1.5 times their nominal value. This restriction implies that the contribution of BESS-GFM to the short-circuit power available at the node is quantitatively limited. The most advanced paper on this limit is *Cross-Forming Control* (Dörfler et al., ETH Zürich, 2024), which proposes hybrid control architectures to avoid current saturation during faults.

### Synchronous Condensers: Short-Circuit and Genuine Rotational Inertia

The current limitation of inverters justifies the complementary deployment of synchronous condensers (SynCons): large-mass rotating machines coupled synchronously but operated in vacuum, freely exchanging reactive power based on the excitation level.

Their contribution is twofold: short-circuit currents on the order of 300–400% of their nominal value during the first cycles, increasing the SCR at the connection node; and genuine rotational inertia physically available in the first millisecond of a transient, without algorithm mediation or control latency.

The Brownfield strategy proposes a deployment path that minimizes investment in new civil works: converting the large alternators of coal, combined cycle, or nuclear plants undergoing decommissioning into synchronous condensers, once the turbine-generator set is decoupled. This conversion transforms stranded assets into systemic stability resources, preserving the value of the already installed 400 kV evacuation nodes.

### Hybrid Architecture: Complementarity as a Condition for Resilience

None of the three technology families, deployed in isolation, is sufficient:

- BESS-GFM provide response speed, control precision, and black-start capability, but are limited in short-circuit current.
- SynCons provide genuine rotational inertia and high short-circuit power, but lack the sub-cyclic agility of power electronics and do not manage active energy.
- Synthetic inertia and FFR maximize the utilization of the battery bank's energy margin, but depend on a minimum nodal stiffness—provided by SynCons—for their algorithms to be effective.

### AI and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for Stability

The gap between the academic state of the art and the operational reality of European TSOs was evident in the Iberian blackout. While control rooms like those of REE or RTE (France) still use classical modal analysis tools to monitor Phasor Measurement Units (PMUs), the research frontier has solved collapse prediction using Deep Learning.

Graph Neural Network (GNN) architectures are inherently topological: they understand the electrical grid not as independent variables, but as an interconnected graph of impedances. Recent mathematical models (e.g., Nauck et al., 2023) apply GNNs to phasor data to predict a node's Stability Basin (SNBS) before voltage begins to drop. Similarly, architectures based on Transformers (like the StaaT model, 2024) have demonstrated significantly outperforming LSTM networks in short-term voltage stability prediction using only PMU time series. Implementing an AI system based on these architectures would have detected the narrowing of the Q-V margin at the Granada node minutes before the transformer thermally collapsed.

### Multi-Agent Swarms for Decentralized Black-Start

The 2025 Iberian black-start required nearly 16 hours to fully restore the peninsula operating under the classic centralized top-down model. The future of supply restoration, especially with the deployment of grid-forming, points towards a distributed model. Using Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), multiple microgrids equipped with GFM can autonomously pre-synchronize (communicating via consensus algorithms) forming "live islands" that subsequently connect to each other. This technique, already tested in pilots like Holy Cross Energy in Colorado (NREL, 2024), has the potential to reduce restoration times from days to mere hours.

## The Normative Response: From Obsolete P.O. 7.4 to Mandatory Grid-Forming

### Deficiencies of the Pre-existing Framework

The P.O. 7.4 in effect at the time of the blackout had not been substantially revised for approximately twenty-five years. It had two critical deficiencies:

1. Participation asymmetry: 82% of the generation fleet operated with a fixed power factor, unable to dynamically inject or absorb reactive power.
2. Dead band: synchronous generators were exempt from acting if voltage remained between 405 kV and 410 kV, configuring a step-wise response structurally incompatible with the speed of the documented capacitive transient.

### Revision of P.O. 7.4, the VOLTAIRE System, and New Remuneration Scheme

The CNMC Resolution of June 12, 2025 (BOE-A-2025-13076) reformulated P.O. 7.4 under the new non-frequency services framework. The fundamental innovation is the replacement of the static setpoint model with a remunerated dynamic provision: precise tracking of real-time setpoints sent telemetrically by CECOEL via CECRE.

The communication channel is articulated through the VOLTAIRE system: a proportional-integral control loop in two hierarchical layers. Tertiary Regulation globally optimizes the voltage profile through reactive optimal power flows. Secondary Regulation sends real-time telemetered setpoints with a 4 s sampling resolution, closing the control loop at the plant scale and eliminating dependence on manual operator response to low-amplitude transients. This architecture transforms the role of the renewable plant: from a passive consumer of grid voltage to an active actuator of the national regulation system.

The associated remuneration includes: 2 €/MVArh for real-time provision during hours with positive net production; and for hours with zero production—the nighttime period when the Ferranti effect of unloaded lines generates overvoltages—a remuneration indexed to the Daily Average Price:

$$
r_Q = 0.05 \times 1.15 \times \mathrm{DAP} + 5 \quad [€/\mathrm{MVArh}]
$$

*(Formula: BOE-A-2025-13076, CNMC Resolution June 12, 2025)*

BESS systems additionally receive an availability payment of 2.7 €/MW/day, conditional on maintaining a valid sample compliance rate above 90%.

Royal Decree 997/2025 complemented the reform with four urgent measures: legal redefinition of BESS as systemic stability service assets; cleanup of the obsolete connection permit registry; mandatory telemetry with resolution < 4 s for all significant IBR plants; and mandatory PSS/POD module in all newly installed GFM inverters with power > 10 MW.

A revealing tension emerged during the process: in October 2025, REE requested raising the compliance requirement from 75% to 90% of the time. The CNMC denied it, arguing the technical impossibility of existing synchronous units to reach that level with the required adjustment ramps. The data is significant in the reverse sense: GFM inverters could easily meet that requirement. The adaptation of the operational framework to the capabilities of IBR-GFM is advancing faster than that of the synchronous assets the standard replaces as providers of ancillary services.

### The European Framework: NC RfG 2.0 and ENTSO-E Phase I and Phase II Reports

The NC RfG in force over the last decade was designed under the GFL paradigm: it standardized fault ride-through profiles and frequency thresholds, but did not contemplate the possibility that no heavy rotating machine would remain to follow. This conceptual deficiency was demonstrated in the 8 seconds of the Iberian collapse.

The ENTSO-E Technical Group (TG GFC) crystallized its work in two documents:

- Phase I Report (May 2024): Fundamental technical definition of GFM required by the new code—"voltage source behind a constant effective reactance" during the first milliseconds. This phasor model de facto prohibits direct dependencies on PLL algorithms for primary control.
- Phase II Report (November 2025): Published under the weight of evidence from the Iberian collapse, with cross-cutting endorsement from CENELEC, WindEurope, SolarPower Europe, and EASE. It establishes taxatively how new inverter-based plants must stabilize the system.

## Essential Reliability Service (ERS) Markets

### The Headroom Problem: Why the Energy Market Does Not Remunerate Stability

For a GFM inverter to act as a voltage source under severe RoCoF or a disturbance, it must mandatorily maintain a reserve of its apparent capacity ($S_{\max}$) unused in steady state. This reserve—the headroom—requires either deliberately limiting the active power injected into the day-ahead market, or oversizing the power stage of its IGBTs, drastically increasing CAPEX. Without direct economic compensation, neither option is commercially viable.

The structural failure of the regulatory architecture until April 28 lay in its punitive approach: penalizing technical deviations without remunerating firmness attributes. ERS correct this asymmetry.

### Three Remuneration Mechanisms

1. Regional synthetic inertia and FFR markets. The design will advance towards auctions where an availability payment is made to assets capable of providing inertial support within < 5 ms margins. The logic of the mechanism is revenue stacking: BESS systems receive simultaneous income from energy arbitrage in the day-ahead market, secondary and tertiary regulation auctions (aFRR/mFRR), and payments for synthetic inertia and FFR capacity. This multiplicity of revenue streams compensates for the high CAPEX of oversizing and the loss of revenue from reactive headroom.

2. Geographic short-circuit level signals. Unlike frequency—a global metric—SCR and overvoltages are radically local phenomena. The vulnerable nodes of April 28 do not coincide with those of highest installed power density. ERS markets will adopt granular spatial price signals: discounted connection tariffs or reactive capacity auctions at perimeter nodes mathematically identified by TSOs as enclaves with SCR < 2. The location of BESS-GFM at the correct nodes becomes decisive for the economic viability of the project.

3. Ex-ante remuneration for SynCons. For infrastructures like SynCons—which provide pure physical inertia and massive short-circuit currents but cannot sell energy in the hourly market—the regulation contemplates Rate of Return Regulation models without exposure to energy price risk, or long-term awards that shield capital investment.

EirGrid (Ireland) and ERCOT (Texas) are the proven operational references towards which European design converges: both operators have faced the challenges of non-synchronous penetrations above 75% ahead of time.

### Evolution of the Spanish Framework in 2026

On May 8, 2026, the CNMC opened a public consultation process for the modification of Operating Procedures P.O. 7.4 and P.O. 14.4 (File DCOOR/DE/006/26), aimed at creating zonal markets for non-frequency services with active participation of IBR and BESS.

The structural lesson of April 28 inscribes into European regulatory design a consequence of greater scope than any incremental adjustment to operating procedures: the physical attributes that synchronous systems inherently provided—inertia, short-circuit power, autonomous voltage control—have ceased to be free externalities and have become services whose provision must be defined, verified, and explicitly remunerated. The pure marginalist energy model is structurally incapable of remunerating these attributes. Without this economic translation of resilience, no technical requirement of NC RfG 2.0 will be sustainable in the medium term.
